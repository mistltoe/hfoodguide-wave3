Introduction

(red mulberry), a native species of North America, plays an important role in maintaining the biodiversity of pristine riparian habitats. It is distributed as far north as Southern Ontario, Canada, extending southward to Southern Florida and as far west as the eastern margin of the Great Plains.is facing a severe threat to its species’ integrity due to taxonomic challenges and introgressive hybridization with the invasive(white mulberry).was introduced to North America in the early 1600s during the colonial period to establish a silk industry. This species has rapidly expanded its range, overlapped, and exceeded the distribution range of, leading to concerns over the survival of the native species.

Hybridization betweenandhas posed genetic and demographic challenges for the conservation of the native. Previous studies found introgressive hybridization betweenand several generations of fertile hybrids withcreating a hybrid/genetic swamp. This has caused taxonomic confusions, even among experts.is listed as an endangered species in Canada and in several U.S. states such as Connecticut and Massachusetts, while it is considered threatened in Michigan and Vermont., with its rapid growth and reproductive advantages, continues to threaten the native species. Consequently, there is an urgent need to study’s genome to develop conservation strategies and prevent further genetic swamping.

Although mulberries have been widely studied for their economic and ecological importance, the specific genetic composition and evolutionary history ofremain relatively underexplored. Previous phylogenetic studies have employed various molecular markers, including nuclear ITS and chloroplastLF regionsto explore the relationships amongspecies. Despite earlier suggestions of a non-monophyletic origin for the genus, recent studies indicate thatspecies are monophyletic, as shown in studies by Gardner et al., Zeng et al., Yang et al., and Wang et al.. To fully resolve the phylogenetic relationships and gain deeper insights into the evolutionary history ofspecies, comprehensive genomic data will be critical in understanding natural relationships that earlier molecular studies have speculated.

Chloroplast genomes, being maternally inherited and highly conserved, offer valuable insights into population genetics, phylogenetics, and the evolution of plants, and serve as a tool for taxonomic clarification and conservation. The major objectives of this study were: (1) to sequence, assemble, and annotate the complete chloroplast genome of, and (2) to perform phylogenomic analyses ofspecies to assess their evolutionary history and hybridization events within the genus. The availability of complete chloroplast genomes ofis crucial for understanding its genetic structure, resolving taxonomic challenges within the genus, and developing conservation strategies.

Codon usage bias

Codon usage analysis revealed thatchloroplast genomes encode approximately 22,775 codons. There was no difference in the codon usage bias within the species. We found 30 codons with RSCU values above one, 32 codons (including three stop codons: UAG, UGA, and UAA) with below one whereas two codons: AUG and UGG encoding Methionine and Tryptophan were found to have RSCU values equal to one. The complete chloroplast genomes of North American native, introducedand their hybrids revealed a quadripartite structure with genome sizes ranging from 159,293 to 159,423 bp. Among the assembled chloroplast genomes, the smallest size (159,293 bp) was of.and hybrids withas maternal parent (GenBank:), whereas the largest (159,423 bp) was of.(GenBank:). Thechloroplast genome was at least 103 bp larger than that of. The assembled chloroplast genomes had almost no variation in the total GC content (36.14–36.15%). The LSC, SSC, and IR regions of thegenome were 88,071, 19,842, and 25,690 bp respectively. Comparative genomic analysis revealed nucleotide insertion in the LSC and deletion in the SSC and IR regions ofcompared to. We did not observe any differences in the number of genes encoded byandchloroplast genomes. In both species, the largest intron (size:: 2,557 bp and: 2,553 bp) was reported to be of thegene embedded within thegene, whereas the smallest intron (sized 16 bp in both) was of. However, we did not observe nucleotide variation in the coding sequences between the two genomes.

The very first chloroplast genome was sequenced in 1986 from the plant(Tobacco) and it was reported to have a size of 156 kilobase pairs (kbp) with 25 kbp IR regions separated by 87 kbp LSC and 19 kbp SSC regions. Since then, hundreds of chloroplast genomes from several species have been sequenced and reported. The first chloroplast genome for the genuswas sequenced in 2006 by Ravi et al.fromvar. K2, and the genome size was reported 158,484 bp. Here, the chloroplast genomes we assembled are larger thanandvar. K2. Nevertheless, they are smaller than thecultivar Tianquan-12 (NCBI:)and(NCBI:)with genome sizes of 159,681 and 160,041 bp, respectively. The assembled red mulberry chloroplast genomes resemble similar genome organization, gene order, and GC content as reported earlier for other mulberries.

The number of genes predicted in our analysis matches with the first reported chloroplast genome by Ravi et al., in, with 128 genes including 83 protein-coding, 37 tRNAs, and 8 rRNAs. However, this number is larger than the genes reported by Zeng et al., who reported only 125 genes, including 81 protein-coding, 8 rRNAs, and 36 tRNAs. The two genes previously reported asandby Ravi et al., were renamed asand. In 1997, Boudreau et al.reported the essence ofandin the accumulation of the Photosystem I (PSI) complex in. Later, in 2011 Wicke et al.proposed to rename the genesandto() and, () which is widely followed. Theandgenes with non-canonical start codon: ACG requiring RNA editing altering to AUG before translation has been well documented in the angiosperm chloroplast genome. The only pseudogene reported in thechloroplast genome was thegene in the IRB region as previously reported. Unlike Ravi et al.who reportedgene into be separated by one base pair from IRB/LSC, we did not observe any insertion in the border region for our constructed genome. However, the extendedgene and presence ofas a pseudogene in() and() indicate a possible Ebby effect (expansion/contraction) in the IR region of thechloroplast genome. Previously, Li et al.have reportedpseudogene in the IR region inand Huang et al.in thechloroplast genome.

Unlike the variable numbers of repeats detected inchloroplast genome, we detected 44 repeats inchloroplast genome. In thechloroplast genome, the 22 bp repeats accounted for the highest frequency (= 12, 16.22%) whereas 24, 26, 28, and 29 bp repeats had a single occurrence. Similarly, palindromic repeats were followed by forward and reverse repeats, with the total number of 24, 12, and 8, respectively. No complement repeats were found in thegenome. Unlikewith 74–76 SSRs, allaccessions had 75 SSRs in the chloroplast genome. The repeat elements are widely distributed in the intron and intergenic-spacer regions of the chloroplast genome. Polymorphic chloroplast microsatellite (SSR) markers have been widely applied in population genetics, not only limited to haplotype identification but also for the detection of intra- and inter-specific variations. In the chloroplast genome of angiosperms, repeat elements are primarily concentrated in the LSC regions. Previously, Kong and Yangdetected 83 SSRs in the chloroplast genome ofandgreater than our reported value. Similarly, the chloroplast SSRs ofandhave been reported to be rich in AT content which is in alignment with our result. The variation in the chloroplast SSRs withindetermined in this study highlights the potential use of these markers in population genetics. Moreover, tandem repeats and SSRs hold significant promise for the interspecific DNA fingerprinting within the genus.

We detected a total of 22,775 codons in thechloroplast genome, whereasgenome had a slightly higher number of codons (22,795). With the overall GC content in the codon of approximately 37% in both genomes, we found a minimal numeral difference in the RCSU analysis. Similar to, AUU encoding Isoleucine accounted for high frequency (984, ca. 4.32%) and UGC encoding Cysteine– the least (57, 0.25%) in’s genome. Intoo, Leucine was the most abundant amino acid (2431 codons, 10.66%) whereas Cysteine was less frequent (260 codons, 1.14%). A codon is described as the triplet of nucleotides coding for a specific amino acid where 64 triplets code for 20 amino acids. If two or more codons encode for the same amino acids, they are known as synonymous codons. The use of synonymous codons does not necessarily alter protein structures; however, mutational biases might influence the usage of synonymous codons. Sharp and Li (1986)describe the relative synonymous codon usage value as “RSCU value for a codon is simply the observed frequency of that codon divided by the frequency expected under the assumption of equal usage of the synonymous codons for an amino acid”. An RSCU value equal to one indicates uniform codon usage particularly in unbiased genes whereas a value greater than one signifies a higher frequency of the codon usage and less than one implies a lower frequency. Kong and Yangreported approximately 26,000 codons in fivespecies with a GC content of ~ 37%, even though the number of codons seems to be higher than what we have reported, the difference is due to our research considering only one gene of the IR region, otherwise, if we consider the duplicated genes the number of codon in our chloroplast genome is ca. 26,000 too. Ravi et al.also reported similar codon composition for, with leucine being the most abundant codon (10.71%) and cysteine the least frequent (1.17%), which is in parallel with our findings. Furthermore, most studies have shown consistent RSCU values across codons signifying the highly conserved nature of the chloroplast genome.

To date, only a limited number of studies have focused on assessing intraspecific variation using chloroplast genomes. This study is among the first ones in the genusto explore population genetics based on the whole chloroplast DNA. While chloroplast markers have been previously implemented inphylogenomics, none of the prior studies have reported intraspecific variation in the markers used. Even though we found variation in the length of the-type genome, the length difference was mostly due to indels in non-coding regions, which is in agreement with Li et al.'s findings forhybrids. The nucleotide polymorphism for(Pi = 0.00002) was comparatively lower than the reported for Castor beans (Pi = 0.00046) by Muraguri et al.. The Pi reported by Wang et al.across theplastome (0.00096) is not in agreement with our finding, here we found the overall Pi across 12plastome to be 0.00319. Moreover, their analysis showed only the-gene region had a Pi > 0.01, however, we found 11 regions with nucleotide diversity above 0.01 holding the potential to develop molecular markers for segregating thespecies. Based on the identical chloroplast genome of, it is plausible to hypothesize that these individuals may have descended from a single population introduced to North America in the early 1600s. Limited generational turnover since their introduction could explain the lack of detectable recombination in their chloroplast genomes. We did not see any chloroplast genome recombination in the potentialhybrids aligning with the results of Park et al.for cucumber, Li et al.forhybrids, and Choi et al.forhybrids showing the maternal origin of the chloroplast genome. The higher nucleotide polymorphism between two species indicates the potential use of chloroplast DNA markers for species identification, however, it does not, alone, guarantee hybrid identification.

Interestingly, higher number of the observed haplotypes in the sampled populations from Nebraska and Kansas maybe attributed to extensive sampling in these regions. In contrast, we noted fewer haplotypes from sparsely distributed individuals across the southern boundaries. This pattern may be partly due to a limited sampling and partly reflect the impact of climate change. Further investigation through extensive sampling is needed to clarify the impact of ecological factors on the species distribution. It is important to note that the species is already endangered, making it challenging to locate sparsely distributed individuals, providing a clearer understanding of the population structure. Statistical modeling performed in this study shows a modest effect of ecological attributes in shaping genetic diversity, supported by the Climate Change Tree Atlas which has modeled and predicted the northward expansion ofhabitat in response to the climatic factors.

The expansion and contraction of IR region – also known as Ebb and Flow events – is one of the factors influencing the change in chloroplast genome size. The study of IR/SC junction more specifically provides insight into the interspecies variation caused due to the changing IR region. Recent research by Huang et al.showed a conserved IR region in thegenus, however, theIR expansion was noted when compared to closely relatedvar.. We noted that the IR region in thespecies was conserved, and the variation was majorly due to indels present between the genomes of the distinct species. This is the first report of IR/SC junction analysis in thegenus.

Fossil records and divergence time analysis of

Fossil records provide valuable insights into the evolutionary history and geographic distribution of plant species, including the genusand its relatives. In 1890s, Nathorstreported the fossilized leaf and fruit ofNathorst for the first time from Greenland dating back to the Cretaceous era. Similarly, in 1983, Mehrotra et al.reported the early Tertiary fossil wood offrom the Deccan Intertrappean beds in India. Multiple studies have placed the origin or crown age ofbetween 42 and 64 MYA. For this reason, we used the chloroplast genome ofas an outgroup in determining the divergence time of 12species included in our analysis. Fossilizedleaves are reported from the Alaskan Cenozoic, New Jersey Pleistocene, and Southern Mississippian middle Miocenein North America. Additionally, fossilizedfruits are reported, from the Miocene to early Eocene in Eurasia. Recently, Patel et al.reported an early Eocene fossilizedleaf from Bikaner, India, representing the earliest fossil evidence in Asia. Gardner et al.and Yang et al.inferred the separation of() and() from otherspecies during the Eocene, which is in alignment with our results. While both studies inferredlineage to be ancestral to both American and Asian clades, our chloroplast genome analysis does not fully support the former inference, and conflicts with the divergence time of the Asian clade. Asian,, andare characterized by long style. The fossil-based divergence time analysis suggested the repeated evolution of long style, probably occurring around 4.5 MYA, simultaneously, in these three species which diverged from their common ancestor approximately 21.34 MYA. This finding aligns well with the suggestion of Nepal, as the long-styled taxa used in the present study do not form a distinct clade.

The fossilizedspecies date back to the early Eocene Epoch of the Cenezoic Era suggesting a Eurasian or Asian origin of the species. However, this does not necessarily suggest the precise origin of individualspecies as fossil records are based on morphological similarities with the extant species. This has limited the study of the biogeographical origin ofspecies. Nevertheless, we cannot rule out the fact that the Last Glacial Maxima (LGM) played an important role in speciation, including that of thespecies. The glaciation events during the Pleistocene Epoch are noted to have a profound effect on the natural ecosystem within the Northern hemisphere. During LGM, the expanded Laurentide ice sheet to 40–50°N in Eurasia and North Americamight have led to the destruction of native species. Oxygen isotope analysis from the deep-sea cores indicates glacial events following a cyclic pattern of long glaciation duration leading to drastic temperature increment with short glacial recession, providing opportunities for the surviving species to fill and colonize the new niches. When a niche is colonized by a population that is available to all individuals in the population, an even and gradual expansion without change in allele frequency across the population range into the niches will occur. However, colonization would influence the genetic structure of future populations when biotic and abiotic factors influence the movement of individuals into a niche away from the source population with a change in allele frequencies. The leading edge sub-populations near the habitation limit of a population would facilitate repopulation of the earlier glaciated area with the future generation expressing similar genetic composition as their colonizing ancestors and the process is known as leading edge expansion. This process could have resulted in the speciation of the differentspecies distributed throughout the world, which then remained isolated from each other’s habitat. Moreover, in North America,, based on competitive exclusion, would have beneficial selectivity for colonizing niches opened by glaciation. Theindividuals in a glacier-induced leading edge expansion of sub-populations would not carry all alleles from the source population, resulting in less genetically diverse future populations and a selective effect on genetic variation. After the dramatic bottleneck effect, the loci with low-frequency alleles within the source population are least likely to remain polymorphic, or once rare alleles from the source population can hold a larger presence within newly colonized populations. Thus, founder effects induced by glaciation may have profoundly shaped the genetic structure ofpopulations. The admixtures of chloroplast haplotypes ofsuggest that isolation and diversification were influenced by repeated glacial maxima during the Pleistocene Epoch.

This study is the first to sequence and analyze the complete chloroplast genome of the endangered(red mulberry), alongside(white mulberry) and their hybrids. These findings offer foundational data on the genome structure of, critical for understanding its evolutionary history and addressing taxonomic complexities within the genus. Phylogenomic analysis revealed distinct clades for Asian, North American, South American, and Africanspecies, supporting the monophyly of the genus. These findings also highlight the evolutionary relationships amongspecies and underscore the complexity of hybridization events within the genus. The identification of chloroplast SSRs and tandem repeats inoffers promising tools for population genetics and interspecific DNA fingerprinting. Expanding sampling across the native range ofwould enable population structure analysis, ecological adaptation, and genetic variation. DNA extraction and sequencing

Approximately 100 mg of silica-dried preserved leaf tissue was subjected to total genomic DNA extraction using DNeasyPlant Mini Kit (Qiagen, USA) following Qiagen’s protocol with slight modifications. The extracted DNA was subjected to gel electrophoresis (1.2% T.A.E.) and quantified using a Nanodrop Spectrophotometer (ThermoFisher). The extracted DNA were sent to CD-Genomics (Shirley, NY, USA) for library preparation and sequencing using Illumina HiSeq™ PE150 sequencing.

assembly of the chloroplast genome

FastQC (v0.11.9) ()program accessed from South Dakota State University’s High Performance Computing (HPC) cluster was utilized to assess the quality of the sequence reads. Trimmomatic (v0.39)was used for trimming the adapter sequence as well as to get rid of low-quality bases (QC < 20) with the options: PE -phred33 ILLUMINACLIP: TruSeq2-PE.fa:2:30:10:2:True LEADING:3 TRAILING:3 MINLEN:36). After quality control of the dataset, MultiQC (v1.13)was used to visualize the results obtained. Then, the quality controlled paired-end data for each read obtained using Trimmomatic from the two lanes were merged. The paired reads were then subjected toassembly using GetOrganelle (v1.7.6.1).

Annotation and visualization

The GetOrganelleassembled chloroplast genome was annotated with GeSeqaccessed through Chloroboxselecting the same orientation of the SSC region as suggested by Zeng et al.. The FASTA file of the assembled genome was uploaded to GeSeq [options: Circular, Sequence source: Plastid (land plants), Annotation Options: Annotate plastid Inverted Repeats (IR), Annotate plastid trans-spliced rps12, Annotation Support: Support annotation by Chloëselecting BLATsearch [Protein search identity: 25%; rRNA, tRNA, DNA search identity: 90%; Annotate: CDS, tRNA, rRNA; Options: Ignore genes annotated as locus tag; MPI-MP Reference Set: chloroplast land plants (CDS + rRNA)] along with HMMERprofile search [Chloroplast land plants; Annotate: CDS + rRNA] and tRNAscan-SE (v2.0.7)[Sequence source: Organellar tRNAs; Genetic Code: Universal, Cut-off score for reporting tRNAs: 15; Score and report output options: Disable pseudogene checking, Display detailed prediction output, Show origin of first-pass hits, Show primary and secondary structure components to scores] for annotation. The annotated output was uploaded to Geneious Primefor manual correction. NCBI blastxsearch was used for the selection of the best hit for multiple annotated genes, and CDS and tRNAscan-SE were considered for the selection of tRNAs (if multiple hits were present). The corrected annotated file was then uploaded to OGDRAWfor the visualization of the circularized chloroplast genome.

Repeat and simple sequence repeat (SSR) analysis

For the detection of forward (f), reverse (r), complement (c), and palindromic (p) repeats, web-based REPuter ()with the default parameters to detect the repeats ranging from 20 to 100 bases was used.

The online version of MISA v2.1() was used for detecting mono-, tri-, tetra-, penta-, and hexanucleotide simple sequence repeats, or microsatellites. The minimum repeat threshold for the detection of palindromic SSRs was set to ten for mononucleotide, five for dinucleotide, four for trinucleotide, and three for tetra-, penta- and hexanucleotides, respectively. Moreover, for the detection of compound SSRs, the maximum length of sequence between two SSRs to register as a compound SSR was set to 50 nucleotides.

Analysis of chlorotypes and ecological factors

Chlorotype network was constructed using the PopART(Population Analysis with Reticulate Trees) v1.7 software using Minimum Spanning Network.

Soil attributes, including pH, organic carbon (dg/kg), and nitrogen (cg/kg) data from SoilGrids 250 m 2.0as well as climatic parameters, including global mean precipitation (mm) and temperature (°C) from WorldClim 2, were accessed through Living Atlas using ArcGIS Pro software. These raster datasets were layered with the GIS coordinates to extract the corresponding mean attributes values. Elevation data was acquired from dcode.fr/earth-elevation (retrieved on 20th January, 2025) using longitude and latitude data. The ecological parameters were normalized using minimum-maximum normalization to employ generalized linear model and multinomial logistic model to observe their effects on haplotype diversity using RStudio 2024.12.0, with the readxland base R v4.3.3packages. The code used for the analysis can be found in Supplemental Material 19.

Divergence time analysis

The Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) method through the Bayesian Evolutionary Analysis Sampling Trees (BEAST) v2.7.6was used for the divergence time analysis.(NCBI Accession:) chloroplast genome was used as an outgroup for the fossil-based molecular clock calibration with the crown age ranging between 60 and 70 MYA. The crown age of thespecies was considered to be 30–45 MYA. The MAFFT-aligned chloroplast genome ofalong with the 12chloroplast, as described previously for the Boundary analysis, was imported to Bayesian Evolutionary Analysis Utility (BEAUti) version 2 within the BEAST package. The use tip dates option was checked with the date specified numerically as the year before the present. The General Time Reversible (GTR) substitution model was selected as suggested by MEGA11with frequencies as estimated along with the rates 1.0 for AC, AG, AT, CG, and GT with the estimate box checked and CT without the estimate box checked.
