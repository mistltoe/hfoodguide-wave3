Introduction

In recent years, there has been an increasing interest in assessing the nutritional makeup and bioactive properties of local fruit species, as environmental and geographical factors can significantly affect their phytochemical profile []. The genus, (family Moraceae) encompasses economically significant species widely cultivated for their nutritional, pharmaceutical, and industrial worth []. Among them, white mulberry (), black mulberry (), and red mulberry () are the most widely recognized species worldwide [].

Mulberry fruits are valued for their sensory attributes and adaptability in fresh consumption and food production sectors, encompassing beverages, preserves, and nutritional food products []. They are rich sources of bioactive compounds such as phenols, flavonoids, anthocyanins, and ascorbic acid, which contribute to their strong antioxidant activity and associated health benefits []. These compounds have been associated with protective effects against cancer, cardiovascular disorders, and diabetes [].

Previous studies have documented significant variations in physicochemical and antioxidant characteristics among mulberry species and genotypes originating from different geographic areas. For instance, Ercisli and Orhan [] identified considerable differences in total phenolic content and antioxidant activity among black, white, and red mulberries, while Arfan et al. [] found that genetic differences had a significant effect on anthocyanin levels and antioxidant potential under different environmental conditions. Geographic and environmental factors, including climate and soil properties, have also been identified as major drivers of mulberry fruit quality [,].

In Iran, research has primarily concentrated onand. Studies by Gharibi et al. [], Hosseini et al. [] and Khadivi et al. [] found significant regional differences in phenolic composition, antioxidant activity and phenotypic diversity among genotypes of these species. In contrast, systematic investigations on.within Iran remain scarce, despite its ecological adaptation and traditional cultivation in several regions. Notably, no integrated study has simultaneously compared physical attributes, biochemical composition, and antioxidant activity of red mulberry genotypes across multiple geographic locations within the country.

Earlier research has indicated considerable variation in the quality of fruit and its antioxidant properties amonggenotypes is largely driven by genetic diversity and environmental heterogeneity [,]. Assessing intra-specific diversity is therefore essential for identifying superior genotypes and supporting breeding, conservation, and sustainable utilization strategies []. Given the growing demand for fruits with enhanced functional and nutraceutical value, identifying antioxidant-rich mulberry genotypes has become increasingly important []. Despite global interest in mulberry phytochemical diversity, information on regional variability of red mulberry genotypes from the Middle East, particularly Iran, is still limited. This knowledge gap constrains the effective use of local genetic resources in breeding programs and value-added product development.

Northwestern Iran is a primary area for growing red mulberry, but there is a lack of scientific information about its genotypes physicochemical and antioxidant properties. Therefore, the present study aimed to (i) quantify the extent of variation in physical, biochemical, and antioxidant traits among 20genotypes collected from five regions of northwestern Iran, and (ii) identify superior genotypes with high potential for fresh consumption, processing, and nutraceutical applications. We hypothesized that significant genotype-dependent variation exists in antioxidant activity and fruit quality traits, reflecting both genetic diversity and regional environmental influences.

This study was conducted in five major fruit-growing regions (Urmia, Khoy, Mahabad, Miandoab, and Oshnavieh) in West Azerbaijan Province, northwestern Iran (Fig.). These areas represent key habitats for red mulberry (L.) cultivation and were selected to encompass a range of agroecological conditions, with sites separated by ≥ 200 km and differing in altitude, temperature, and precipitation (Table). Field sampling and phenotypic evaluations were carried out during the commercial ripening period in July 2024 for six months.

Genotype selection and authentication

A total of twentygenotypes were selected for analysis using a purposive sampling strategy designed to capture phenotypic diversity. Within each region, four representative genotypes were chosen based on distinct and stable morphological and pomological traits, including tree vigor, fruit size, fruit color, and yield performance. To ensure species purity and minimize confounding effects, selection was restricted to mature, healthy trees with consistent fruiting behavior over multiple seasons; individuals exhibiting ambiguous traits or potential hybrid characteristics were excluded.

Fig. 1

Collection sites of red mulberry genotypes studied in five regions of West Azerbaijan Province (WAP): 1. Khoy, 2. Urmia, 3. Oshnavieh, 4. Mahabad, 5. Miandoab

Species identification followed a two-step verification process: (1) initial field identification using standard taxonomic keys based on leaf morphology, bark characteristics, fruit traits, and growth habit; and (2) final authentication by a taxonomic specialist (Prof. Hamid Hassanpour, Department of Horticultural Sciences, Urmia University). Voucher specimens were deposited in the departmental herbarium (accession no. 1578).

Sampling rationale and scope

The selected genotypes represent locally recognized, distinct populations rather than a random sample of the entire regional population. This approach was adopted to provide meaningful insight into regional diversity while maintaining analytical depth and feasibility for detailed physicochemical profiling. Although the study is geographically focused within northwestern Iran, the deliberate selection of sites with distinct environmental parameters (Table) ensures that the sampled germplasm reflects meaningful agroecological variation, despite sharing a broader macroclimatic context.

Sample collection

For each genotype, fully ripe fruits were collected at commercial maturity, defined by standard maturity indices including complete development of species-specific color, full pigmentation, and soft texture, ensuring uniform maturity stage across all genotypes. A total of approximately 40 Fruits from each genotype were randomly collected from four sides of the trees in three sets of replicates, placed in individual polyethylene bags sealed with a zip lock, and transported in a refrigerated tank filled with crushed ice to prevent damage. 20 fully expanded, healthy leaves were also collected from the mid-canopy region. Sample size was determined based on analytical requirements and previous studies on mulberry fruit biochemical characterization, ensuring sufficient material for all physicochemical and antioxidant assays while maintaining consistency across genotypes. All the samples were transferred to the laboratory for subsequent physicochemical and biochemical analyses.

Morphological measurements

The dimensions of the leaves and fruits (length, width, petiole length, and thickness) were measured with a digital caliper (model, precision of ± 0.001 mm). The weight of the fruit was measured using a digital balance (model Candg 1300, accuracy of ± 0.001 g).

Fruit skin color measurement

The color of the fruits was measured with a colorimeter (model Sunset H1149, Japan) at two opposite points of each fruit. Color parameters L*, a*, and b* were recorded, and the hue angle (h°= tan[b/a]) and chroma (C= [a² + b²] ¹ᐟ²) were calculated in accordance with Pathare et al. [].

Measurement of pH, titratable acidity (TA), and total soluble solids (TSS)

Preparation of extract

The fruits were ground in a porcelain mortar using liquid nitrogen. Four grams of the powder were diluted with distilled water to 16 ml, vortexed for 3 to 4 min, and then centrifuged at 10,000 rpm for 20 min at 20 °C. The supernatant was used for subsequent analyses []

The TSS was also measured using a handheld digital refractometer (ATAGO, Japan), which was calibrated with distilled water before each reading.

Measurement of antioxidant parameters

Preparation of extract

A frozen sample weighing 1 g was powdered using liquid nitrogen and then extracted with 10 mL of 80% methanol. The mixture was vortexed vigorously for 1 min, then left to incubate at room temperature for 1 h, mixed again, and centrifuged at 10,000 rpm at 4 °C for 5 min. The extracts were then stored at −20 °C for subsequent biochemical analyses [].

Measurement of total phenol (TP)

The TP content was assessed using the Folin–Ciocalteu method, as described by Amini Khoshalani et al. []. Specifically, 30 µL of the extract was combined with 90 µL of distilled water and 600 µL of 10% Folin reagent. Following a 6-min interval, 480 µL Na₂CO₃ solution was added, and the samples were left in the dark for 2 h. Absorbance was measured at 765 nm, and the TP content was reported in terms of mg gallic acid (GAE) equivalents per 100 g of FW.

Measurement of total flavonoid (TF)

The TF content was determined in accordance with Ercisli & Orhan []. The 200 µL extract was sequentially mixed with 150 µL of NaNO₂, then 300 µL of 10% AlCl₃, and finally 1 mL of 1 M NaOH, at intervals of 5 min. The final volume was brought to 5 mL by adding distilled water, and the absorbance was measured at 510 nm. Results were expressed as mg of catechin (Cat) per 100 g FW.

Measurement of antioxidant activity (AA)

DPPH (2,2-diphenyl-1-picrylhydrazyl)

FRAP (Ferric reducing antioxidant power)

The ferric reducing antioxidant power (FRAP) was determined as described by Dzah et al. []. Briefly, acetate buffer (300 mM), 2,4,6-tri(2-pyridyl) s-triazine (TPTZ) solution (10 mM) in HCl (40 mM) and freshly prepared FeCl3 × 6 H2O solution (10 mM) were mixed in a ratio of 10:1:1. Then 50 µL of extract was added to 3.5 mL of FRAP solution, incubated for 10 min at 37 °C, and absorbance was read at 593 nm. Results were expressed as mg Fe²⁺ SO₄ per g FW.

Measurement of total anthocyanin (TAC)

The TAC content was determined in accordance with Giusti and Wrolstad [] by a pH differential method with minor modifications. First, two buffers with pH values of 1 and 4.5 were prepared, then 100 µL of the supernatant solution was added to 2.5 mL of the buffer with pH 1, and the spectrophotometer (Model Pharmacia LKB) was used to read the absorbance at 530 and 700 nm. Measurement of ascorbic acid (AsA)

Ascorbic acid content was determined according to Klein & Perry [] method. To be specific, 100 µL of fruit powder was blended with 10 mL of 1% metaphosphoric acid; 1 mL of this mixture was combined with 9 mL of 50 µM 2,6-dichloroindophenol, and the absorbance was measured at 515 nm. Results were expressed as mg per 100 mL of FW.

Measurement total soluble carbohydrates (TSC)

The TSC content was determined according to Irigoyen et al. []. Five grams of fruit were mixed with 95% ethanol and then the supernatant was re-extracted with 70% ethanol. Following centrifugation at 4500 rpm for 15 min, 100 µL of extract was then combined with 3 mL of a freshly prepared anthrone reagent (containing 150 mg of anthrone in 100 mL of 72% H₂SO₄) and boiled for 10 min. Absorbance measurements were taken at 625 nm and the results were presented as mg per g of FW.

Statistical analysis

All data were analyzed using the SAS 9.4 software. Mean comparisons were performed using Duncan’s multiple range test at a significance level of≤ 0.05. Principal Component Analysis (PCA) was performed to visualize the overall variation and relationships among genotypes and traits using Statgraphics 19 × 64. Prior to PCA, a Pearson correlation matrix was generated for all quantitative variables using SPSS 27. All original variables were retained for the PCA to preserve the full biological interpretability of the components and to avoid the potential loss of agronomically relevant information that might be masked by simple pairwise correlation []. Also, hierarchical clustering was conducted using Statgraphics 19 × 64.

Results and discussion

Descriptive statistics of the 30 measured traits across thegermplasm are provided in Table. The results revealed extensive phenotypic and biochemical diversity, providing a quantitative foundation for the subsequent genotype-specific analysis. The range and coefficient of variation (CV) were key criteria for investigating the potential for selection within the population. Remarkable variability (CV > 25%) was seen for several agronomically and nutritionally critical attributes. Notably, fruit weight (FWe, CV = 26.4%), total phenol (TP, CV = 38.1%), total flavonoid (TF, CV = 42.7%), and antioxidant activity (DPPH, CV = 43.3%; FRAP, CV = 44.3%) exhibited high CV values. This shows a rich genetic basis and a strong potential for direct phenotypic selection to enhance yield and nutraceutical value []. Petiole length (PL, CV = 26.6%) and fruit volume (V, CV = 31.1%) also revealed high variability, further highlighting the morphological diversity present. In contrast, several quality traits revealed low to moderate variability. Fruit shape index (FL/FWi, CV = 7.9%), sphericity (S, CV = 5.1%), and total soluble solids (TSS, CV = 6.6%) were highly uniform across genotypes. This exhibit these attributes are under potent genetic stabilization or are less influenced by the environmental conditions of the study, which is advantageous for maintaining consistent fruit quality in breeding programs [].

The color coordinates revealed extreme variability, especially for the redness (a*, CV = 54.0%) and yellowness (b*, CV = 54.5%) indices. The hue angle and chroma also indicated high CVs (> 45%), reflecting a broad spectrum of fruit pigmentation from light red to deep purple. This wide variation is directly related to differential anthocyanin accumulation and serves as a visual marker for selecting genotypes with intense, marketable fruit color []. The data confirms that the initial source of antioxidant diversity in this germplasm is phenolic metabolism. The high CVs for TP, TF, and antioxidant assays contrast with the lower variability in ascorbic acid (AsA, CV = 13.6%). This pattern recommends that breeding for improved antioxidant activity should focus on the phenolic pathway rather than AsA content. The concurrent high variability in fruit biomass (FWe, V) and phytochemical concentration (TP, TF) sets the stage for recognizing potential resource allocation trade-offs—a common phenomenon in fruit crops where investment in growth may compete with secondary metabolite production []. The notable ranges (e.g., TP: 249.46–1156.96 mg/100 g) support that the germplasm contains genotypes at both extremes, enabling the study of this relationship and the selection of rare genotypes that may successfully combine high yield with high phytochemical density. In conclusion, the descriptive statistics confirm the studied collection as a valuable and diverse genetic resource. The high heritability implied for many parameters through their large CVs makes this population suitable for effective selection. The subsequent genotype-wise analysis is justified and targeted at harnessing this variability to recognize elite candidates with optimal combinations of these diverse parameters for cultivar development.

Genetic diversity among genotypes is likely reflected in the variation of leaf morphological traits, which adapt to regional environmental conditions such as temperature, humidity, and light intensity. Larger leaves can increase light capture and photosynthetic productivity, resulting in better production of primary and secondary metabolites []. Excessive leaf enlargement under high radiation may also increase transpiration and thermal load, implying that ideal leaf size is contingent on ecological balance rather than absolute dimensions.

Peris et al. [] observed similar morphological variability, which included significant differences in leaf characteristics among five cultivars ofin Kenya, with maximum measurements of 17.78 cm for leaf length, 14.00 cm for leaf width, and 3.52 cm for petiole length. These results are consistent with the present findings, emphasizing the significant effect of genotype × environment interactions on mulberry leaf architecture. Leaf morphology also impacts physiological performance, with broader leaves generally found to have higher chlorophyll content and gas exchange capacity; conversely, petiole length influences leaf orientation and light distribution within the canopy []. The larger leaf sizes seen in Rma3, Ro4, and Rmn1 imply that these cultivars may have better photosynthetic performance, which in turn could result in higher fruit biochemical quality [].

Fruit length, width, thickness, weight, and pedicel length

Statistically significant differences were found among red mulberry genotypes (< 0.01) in the characteristics of fruit length, width, thickness, weight, and pedicel length. Still, no variation was observed in fruit length/width ratio (Table). Fruit length and fruit weight ranged from 18.44 to 28.59 mm and 1.53 to 3.97 g, respectively, with genotype Ru2 exhibiting the greatest fruit length and weight. In contrast, fruit width, fruit thickness and pedicel length varied from 10.63 to 16.48 mm, 11.10 to 17.02 mm and 7.13 to 11.64 mm, respectively, with genotype Ru1 exhibiting the highest fruit width, thickness, and pedicel length. The highest FL/FWi ratio (1.9) was observed in the Rk1 genotype, which ranged from 1.64 to 1.9.

The observed variability is thought to be a reflection of genetic diversity and local adaptation, primarily due to the fact that fruit growth and shape are largely determined by cell division, expansion and hormonal regulation during fruit development. Generally, larger fruit size and weight are linked with improved sink strength and increased carbohydrate accumulation efficiency []. Breeding programs can benefit from this approach in terms of boosting yield and aligning with market demand. Reports of comparable variation in fruit morphology have been found inandgenotypes [], indicating that the differences amonggenotypes in this study probably arise from both genetic and environmental interactions. Moreover, the pedicel length, which was the longest in Ru1, could impact harvest handling and mechanical processing.

Sphericity, geometric mean diameter, marketability coefficient, surface area, fruit volume, and density of fruits

Analysis of variance showed a statistically significant difference (< 0.01) among the twenty red mulberry genotypes in terms of sphericity, geometric mean diameter, marketability coefficient, surface area, fruit volume, and density. The fruit sphericity ranged from 65.86 to 74.67%, and the marketability coefficient of the fruit was recorded from 52.58 to 63.4, with genotype Ru2 exhibiting the highest sphericity and genotype Ro4 exhibiting the highest marketability coefficient. In contrast, fruit geometric mean diameter and fruit volume varied from 13.05 to 19.78 mm and 1.16 to 4.05 cm³, respectively, with genotype Ru1 exhibiting the greatest geometric mean diameter and fruit volume, suggesting its suitability for fresh consumption and processing applications. This also reflects marked differences in fruit size and shape among genotypes. Rk1 showed the highest density, which ranged from 0.09 to 0.14 g/cm³; this parameter is often associated with firmer texture and potentially improved postharvest handling performance. while fruit surface area varied from 5.35 to 12.2 cm, with genotypes Ru1 and Ro1 exhibiting the highest surface area (Tablesand). The morphological characteristics of the evaluated genotypes correspond with findings reported forfruit in previous studies [,]. Specifically, the fruit weight range observed aligns with that documented in Turkish cultivars (4.18–5.05 g) by Ercisli & Orhan [], indicating a consistent expression of this trait across geographically distinct germplasm. Furthermore, the broad spectrum of fruit size and density parameters in the present study is consistent with the significant phenotypic diversity reported forgenotypes across various agroecological regions. This corroborates the substantial influence of both genetic background and environmental conditions in shaping the morphological architecture within the genus. These parameters are vital for postharvest engineering uses, including the design of machinery for grading and sorting, as well as the improvement of packaging systems []. Fruits with a higher density often exhibit firmer tissue and enhanced shelf stability, while their sphericity and surface area play a crucial role in regulating airflow and cooling efficiency in storage [].

Fruit color parameters

A statistically significant differences (< 0.01) were observed among genotypes in color parameters (a*, b*, L*, chroma, and Hue). The L* value of fruits ranged from 12.82 to 18.52, with genotype Rk2 exhibiting the highest L* value, which suggests a lighter skin tone in this genotype. At the same time, Rmn1 had the highest a* value and chroma, which varied from 0.47 to 3.92 and 0.75 to 4.13, respectively, indicating a richer red pigmentation in this genotype. The hue value ranged from 0.25 to 0.97, with genotype Ru1 exhibiting the highest hue value. In contrast, the b* value varied from 0.37 to 19.43, with genotype Ru2 exhibiting the highest b* value (Table). The color parameters (L*, a*, b*) determined in this study are consistent with values reported forfrom Turkey (L*: 16.48, a*: 14.20, b*: 3.82) [] and align with findings forandfruits in other regions [,]. Furthermore, the observed hue and chroma ranges correspond with values documented for[]. The significant variability in color indices among our genotypes mirrors patterns described across diverse wild edible fruits []. Fruit color intensity is primarily governed by anthocyanin concentration and its degradation kinetics during maturation and postharvest storage. Environmental factors, particularly sunlight exposure and temperature, critically regulate anthocyanin biosynthesis [], while oxidative and enzymatic processes drive pigment degradation during senescence []. Within this framework, the Rmn1 genotype, distinguished by its superior a* value and chroma, exhibits traits associated with enhanced pigment stability and likely greater visual appeal for fresh markets. The pronounced variation in color indices among the studied germplasm underscores a combined influence of genetic predisposition and environmental adaptation on final fruit coloration.

pH, titratable acidity (TA) and total soluble solids (TSS) of fruits

A statistically significant differences were found for pH (< 0.01), titratable acidity (TA;< 0.05), and total soluble solids (TSS;< 0.01) of fruits. The pH value ranged from 3.23 to 4.95, TSS ranged from 13.53 to 16.17%, and TA varied from 0.70 to 1.61%. TA content was the highest in genotype Rmn4, and genotype Ro4 showed the highest TSS. In contrast, the lowest pH value was found in Ru4 (Table). The pH, titratable acidity (TA), and total soluble solids (TSS) observed in this study correspond with ranges reported for otheraccessions, such as pH ~ 4.04, TA ~ 1.37%, and TSS ~ 15.9% in Turkish germplasm [,]. In contrast, substantially different ranges (TSS: 18.5–19.2%; TA: 0.28–0.68%; pH: 3.70–4.25) have been documented by Parida and Rayaguru []. This pronounced disparity is likely attributable to the strong influence of both genotype-specific metabolic profiles and distinct agro-environmental growing conditions, a key factor in fruit biochemical composition as highlighted by Kattil et al. []. These parameters define the taste and appearance balance and post-harvest quality of mulberries. As fruits ripen, the levels of organic acids decrease due to respiration and conversion into sugars, resulting in higher TSS and lower TA []. As a result, the sugar–acid ratio functions as an indicator of both sweetness perception and maturity stage []. The higher TSS content present in Ro4 indicates improved sugar accumulation capacity and potential for use in processing and fresh market applications.

Total phenol (TP) and total flavonoid (TF)

A statistically significant difference (< 0.01) in TP content was observed among genotypes (Fig.a), with Ru1 having the highest concentration (941.13 mg GAE·100 g⁻¹ FW) and Rmn1 the lowest (268.06 mg GAE·100 g⁻¹ FW). Phenolic compounds act as the primary antioxidants, eliminating reactive oxygen species (ROS) and binding to transition metals []. Typically, higher phenolic content correlates with enhanced antioxidant activity and stress resistance []. The variability among different genotypes is probably due to a combination of genetic factors and the environmental conditions that affect the production of secondary metabolites. The total phenolic (TP) content observed in the present germplasm aligns with the lower to mid-range values reported forin other studies, such as 210.4 mg GAE·100 g⁻¹ FW [] and 435 mg GAE·100 g⁻¹ FW []. The substantial variability in TP content across our genotypes corroborates patterns described for diverse wild edible fruits []. Notably, this variability exists within a spectrum that extends to significantly higher values (1005–2388 mg GAE·100 g⁻¹ FW) reported for otheraccessions [], underscoring the profound impact of genotype, agro-climatic conditions, and cultivation practices on phenolic biosynthesis []. Within this context, genotype Ru1, distinguished by its superior TP content, is identified as a prime candidate for breeding programs targeting enhanced antioxidant capacity in mulberry cultivars. Furthermore, the clustering of TP values among genotypes from the same geographical region suggests that local environmental factors and potential genetic adaptation significantly shape the phenolic profile of the fruit.

A statistically significant difference (< 0.01) was also observed among genotypes for TF (Fig.b). Ro3 showed the highest TF, with 424.25 mg Cat·100 g⁻¹ FW, and Rma3 exhibited the lowest, at 152.16 mg Cat·100 g⁻¹ FW. The total flavonoid (TF) content determined in this study corresponds with values reported forin other regions, such as approximately 219 mg 100 g⁻¹ FW in Turkish germplasm [] and 192 mg 100 g⁻¹ by Dimitrijevic et al. Antioxidant activity

DPPH radical scavenging activity

Analysis of variance revealed a statistically significant difference (< 0.05) among the red mulberry genotypes for the ability to scavenge DPPH radicals. The highest DPPH inhibition, which was 92.87%, was observed in genotype Ru2, whereas the lowest value of 23.12% was found in Ru4 (Fig.a). The antioxidant activity, as measured by DPPH inhibition in the present study, is consistent with the 78.5% inhibition reported for red mulberry fruits by Krishna et al. []. The significant variability observed across our genotypes aligns with the patterns of DPPH-based antioxidant capacity documented in other wild edible fruits by Rymbai et al. []. The variation observed among genotypes may be due to differences in phenolic composition, maturity stage, and environmental adaptation, all of which directly impact antioxidant potential. Genotypic variations have been observed in mulberry and other small fruits, with genotypes showing higher levels of pigmentation also exhibiting enhanced radical scavenging activity [,].

Ferric reducing antioxidant power (FRAP)

Analysis of variance indicated a statistically significant difference (< 0.05) in FRAP values among the different genotypes. Fig.b shows that Ru4 had the highest antioxidant activity based on FRAP (2.86 mg Fe²⁺ SO₄ g⁻¹ FW), whereas Rk2 had the lowest at 0.929 mg Fe²⁺ SO₄ g⁻¹ FW, with FRAP being a measure of how well fruit extracts can donate electrons to reduce ferric ions, a process largely influenced by polyphenol and other reducing compounds concentrations []. Biological macromolecules like lipids, proteins, carbohydrates, and nucleic acids are protected by antioxidants that neutralize reactive oxygen species (ROS). Previous research in mulberry and other berries has shown considerable variation in reducing power among different genotypes and species []. The antioxidant activity based on FRAP assay observed in the present germplasm is consistent with values reported for, specifically 5.4 µmol TE g⁻¹ by Krishna et al. [] and 6.6 µmol TE g⁻¹ by Ozgen et al. []. The inherent variability in FRAP-based antioxidant capacity among our genotypes further aligns with patterns documented across diverse wild edible fruits []. Notably, genotype Ru2, which exhibited the highest antioxidant activity in both DPPH and FRAP assays, emerges as a particularly promising candidate for development in the nutraceutical and functional food sectors.

Total anthocyanin content (TAC)

Analysis of variance revealed statistically significant variations (< 0.01) in TA content among different genotypes. As depicted in Fig.a, Ro4 yielded the highest TAC value of 0.499 mg gFW, whereas the lowest TAC value of 0.209 mg gFW was recorded in Rmn3.The total anthocyanin content (TAC) observed in this study aligns with previous reports on. Parida and Rayaguru [] reported a TAC of 12.6 mg 100 g⁻¹ FW in red mulberry, which falls within the range of values in the present work. This range is consistent with the broader variability (3–200 µg g⁻¹ FW) documented across mulberry germplasm by Ozgen et al. [], underscoring the characteristically higher anthocyanin accumulation in red varieties compared to white ones. Furthermore, the degree of TAC variability within our germplasm corroborates findings for other wild edible fruits, as noted by Rymbai et al. []. The dominant water-soluble flavonoid pigments, anthocyanins, are key contributors to the red and purple coloration of mulberry fruits and also play a significant role in antioxidant defense mechanisms. The accumulation is highly sensitive to environmental stimuli including light intensity, temperature, and oxygen availability []. The current results indicate that genotype Ro4 may have a greater potential for use in nutraceutical and functional foods because of its high concentration of anthocyanin.

Ascorbic acid (AsA) content

The analysis of variance showed statistically significant differences in AsA content among genotypes (< 0.01). Fig.b illustrates that Ru3 and Ru4 exhibited the highest AsA concentration at 21.16 mg 100 mLFW, whereas Rmn4 recorded the lowest at 12.95 mg 100 mLFW. This finding is significantly lower than the findings of Kattil et al. [], which reported an AsA content of red mulberry fruits ranging from 69.80 to 87.49 mg 100 mL. The concentration of AsA in fruit tissues is mainly affected by genotype, environmental conditions, cultivation practices, and maturity stage []. Our results align more closely with ascorbate levels documented in otherspecies (11.9–18.9 mg 100 mL⁻¹) [,] and are consistent with reports on various wild edible fruits []. Vitamin C, or ascorbic acid, functions as a crucial non-enzymatic antioxidant that works in conjunction with enzymatic systems to neutralize ROS and preserve redox balance []. The current study’s outcomes align with previous research, showing that specific red mulberry genotypes, notably Ru3, Ru4 and Ro4, are abundant in natural antioxidants and could be viable options for breeding programs focused on improving fruit nutritional content.

Total soluble carbohydrates (TSC)

An analysis of variance showed a statistically significant difference (< 0.01) in TSC content among the studied red mulberry genotypes. The highest TSC value recorded was 7.07 mg g⁻¹ FW in the Ru2 genotype, whereas the lowest value, at 5.16 mg g⁻¹ FW, was observed in Rmn3 (Fig.). Similar findings for red mulberry fruits have been reported by Skender and Becirspahic [] and Kattil et al. []. Furthermore, the variability in TSC content observed in our study aligns with reports on several wild edible fruits by Rymbai et al. []. Significant differences in TSC content among different genotypes imply a substantial impact of both genetic background and regional environmental factors on carbohydrate metabolism in mulberry fruits. The accumulation of carbohydrates is crucial in establishing the sweetness and overall flavor of fruits, attributes that are fundamental for determining consumer preference and market value []. The main soluble sugars in mulberry fruit (sucrose, fructose, glucose, and sorbitol) contribute to its quality and ripening, with their respective amounts playing a significant role []. The improved TSC in Ru2 could be due to more efficient photosynthesis or increased carbohydrate transport, which may be related to its larger fruit size and enhanced antioxidant properties. The low TSC content in Rmn3 may be associated with suboptimal climatic conditions or discrepancies in the sink-source balance during ripening. The variability in sugar accumulation based on genotype underscores the need to select lines that have both desirable sweetness and biochemical quality for breeding and commercializing red mulberry cultivars. Our results regarding fruit TSC also show that there is no significant difference between genotypes in each region, indicating that regional geography and environmental conditions do not affect fruit TSC.

Correlation analysis

Correlation analysis revealed obvious and biologically significant interrelationships not only within but also between morphological and biochemical trait groups in red mulberry genotypes (Fig.), providing insight into the physiological links between fruit characteristics and metabolic composition. Significant positive links were found among characteristics related to fruit size, such as length, width, and weight, where the correlation coefficient exceeded 0.75, showing that fruit growth is well-coordinated and implies these factors are controlled by closely related developmental processes. Similar associations among morphological traits have been reported in mulberry and other berry species, reflecting shared control over fruit enlargement and biomass accumulation [,,].

Overall, the correlation analysis demonstrated that selected morphological and color traits, particularly hue angle and fruit size indices, were significantly associated with antioxidant-related biochemical parameters. This finding indicates that easily measurable external fruit characteristics can partially predict the internal phytochemical and antioxidant status of red mulberry genotypes.

Strong interconnections among fruit size, geometry, and related biochemical characteristics are indicated by the high positive values of these variables, highlighting the significant role of morphological features in determining overall variability (Table).

Principal component analysis offered a comprehensive interpretation of the intricate connections among morphological, biochemical, and antioxidant-related characteristics in the examined red mulberry genotypes. The prominence of fruit size parameters and antioxidant-related biochemical variables in the first principal components suggests that these characteristics are the main contributors to phenotypic variability. Research on mulberry and other berry crops has revealed patterns where morphological fruit attributes and compounds related to phenolics account for most of the observed variation among different genotypes [,].

The high positive correlations of total phenols, flavonoids, anthocyanins, antioxidant activity indices, and ascorbic acid with the main components indicate a coordinated regulation of secondary metabolism. Phenolic compounds are well-documented to play a central role in antioxidant defense, pigmentation, and stress adaptation, and their close association with antioxidant activity has been extensively documented in small fruits [,]. The co-loading of these biochemical traits with fruit size characteristics suggests that genotypes with improved antioxidant profiles are also likely to display superior fruit morphology, a preferable combination for both nutritional quality and market acceptance.

Furthermore, the separation of genotypes along the major principal components indicates underlying genetic differences affecting fruit development and metabolic pathways. The multivariate structure underscores the significance of combining morphological and biochemical characteristics when assessing genetic diversity. The PCA results offer a stable foundation for pinpointing key characteristics that can be focused on in breeding programs aimed at enhancing fruit quality, antioxidant activity, and overall performance of red mulberry genotypes.

Cluster analysis

Twenty red mulberry genotypes were grouped into four main clusters by cluster analysis, which indicated distinct genetic and geographical differences (Fig.). Genotypes from the same geographic area were found to have the most similar physical, qualitative, and biochemical characteristics, indicating the impact of local environmental and genetic factors on fruit characteristics. The first cluster consisted of genotypes Ru1, Ru2, Ru3, and Ru4, which demonstrated exceptional performance in fruit size, weight, and antioxidant activity relative to other groups. The Urmia region is home to genetically robust lines with both morphological and biochemical benefits, implying its suitability for breeding programs. The second cluster included Ro1, Ro2, Ro3, and Ro4, with Ro1 exhibiting distinct behavior because of reduced TSS and TP values. The variability within the region indicates either localized microclimatic effects or interactions between soil and nutrients. In the third cluster, Rk1 and Rk2 were more similar to Rk3 and Rk4, and together formed a subgroup that was homogeneous, characterized by moderate fruit size and medium antioxidant content. The fourth cluster comprised Rmn1, Rmn2, Rmn3, Rmn4, Rma1, Rma2, Rma3, and Rma4 genotypes, with Rmn1 showing the greatest divergence, showing its significantly lower phenolic and flavonoid concentrations. The separation suggests a significant environmental or genetic drift influence within the Miandoab population. The genotypes Rma1 and Rma2 demonstrated some similarity with the Miandoab group (Rmn3 and Rmn4), suggesting a potential genetic exchange or common origin between the Mahabad and Miandoab areas.

The concordance between PCA and cluster analysis highlights the key influence of fruit biochemical composition and morphological traits on genetic diversity. Cluster analysis at different hierarchical levels helped to clarify the relationships between the red mulberry genotypes by categorizing them based on their physical and chemical attributes. The clustering pattern closely matched the PCA results, supporting the accuracy of the multivariate analyses and establishing that the observed groupings held biological significance rather than being statistically coincidental. Widespread concordance between PCA and cluster analysis has been documented in numerous fruit crop characterization studies [,,]. Genotypes from the same geographical area tend to group together, implying that both genetic background and local environmental factors have a combined effect on fruit trait expression. Temperature, soil type, and water supply are known to interact with genetic factors to influence the biochemical composition of fruit, particularly the accumulation of phenolics and antioxidant properties. Local adaptation likely plays a substantial role in shaping fruit quality characteristics in red mulberry populations [].

Clusters were distinguished based on variations in fruit size, antioxidant properties, and phenolic content, underscoring the significance of biochemical characteristics, notably total phenols, flavonoids, and anthocyanins, as major differentiating features. Berry fruits have been consistently identified by Skrovankova et al. [] as reliable indicators of both their nutritional and functional quality. These attributes enable a clear separation of clusters, which indicates that cluster analysis can be effectively used to identify elite genotypes with desirable combinations of fruit quality traits. The cluster analysis offers valuable practical insights for germplasm management and breeding strategies overall. Groups of genotypes with superior antioxidant properties and desirable fruit forms can be given priority for cultivar development, with genetically separate clusters serving as valuable sources of diversity for future improvement plans.

Conclusions

This study demonstrated significant physicochemical and antioxidant variability amonggenotypes from northwestern Iran. Fruit color attributes, particularly the Hue index, showed the strongest association with antioxidant activity, indicating their suitability as rapid quality indicators. Total anthocyanin content proved to be the most effective trait for discriminating superior genotypes. Among all evaluated genotypes, Ru1 consistently exhibited superior fruit size, antioxidant properties, and overall quality, highlighting its potential for breeding and commercial use. These results confirm the value of integrating morphological, colorimetric, and biochemical traits for the efficient selection of high-quality red mulberry genotypes.
