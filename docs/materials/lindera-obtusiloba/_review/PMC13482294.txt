Introduction

Atopic dermatitis (AD) is a chronic, relapsing inflammatory skin disorder characterized by pruritic eczematous lesions and epidermal barrier dysfunction, arising from a complex interplay of genetic predisposition, environmental triggers, and immune dysregulation. Beyond its high prevalence, particularly in industrialized countries, AD imposes a substantial burden due to impaired quality of life and frequent comorbidities, such as allergic rhinitis and asthma. The pathogenesis of AD is multifaceted, with epidermal barrier disruption acting as both an initiating and amplifying factor of cutaneous inflammation. Loss of barrier integrity increases transepidermal water loss and facilitates the penetration of allergens, irritants, and microbes, thereby perpetuating chronic skin inflammation. Keratinocytes at disrupted sites actively release pro-inflammatory mediators, including interleukin (IL)−8 and thymic stromal lymphopoietin (TSLP), which contribute to the recruitment and activation of immune cells within the skin microenvironment. TSLP activates dendritic cells to drive type 2 T helper cell (Th2) polarization, whereas IL-8 attracts neutrophils and exacerbates tissue injury. Consequently, lesional skin becomes dominated by a Th2-biased immune response marked by elevated IL-4 and IL-13, which impair keratinocyte differentiation, downregulate barrier proteins such as filaggrin, and sustain inflammation. Although type 2 immune responses are central to AD pathogenesis, AD lesions also involve broader cytokine-mediated inflammatory networks, including keratinocyte activation, innate immune signaling, and Th1-associated mediators, particularly depending on disease stage and inflammatory context. Recent transcriptomic analyses further indicate that the progression from non-lesional to acute and chronic AD involves overlapping Th2, Th1, Th17, IL-36, and keratinocyte-associated inflammatory signatures, supporting the interpretation of AD as a complex cytokine-network disease rather than a single-axis Th2 disorder. Therefore, experimental approaches that assess both Th2-related epithelial responses and IFN-γ/TNF-α-driven inflammatory activation may provide a more comprehensive understanding of cutaneous inflammation. This view is also supported by HaCaT optimization studies showing that AD-related gene-expression changes involve not only Th2-associated cytokines but also innate and Th1-associated inflammatory cytokines, including TNF-α and IFN-γ.

Conventional therapies including topical corticosteroids, calcineurin inhibitors, and biologics targeting type-2 cytokines are clinically effective but limited by safety concerns with long-term use, cost, and variable access or response. These limitations motivate the search for safe interventions that can address the multifactorial nature of AD. Natural products are attractive in this regard because of their multi-target pharmacology, long human use, and favorable safety profiles. Many plant-derived constituents (e.g., flavonoids, lignans, coumarins) exhibit anti-inflammatory and antioxidant activities relevant to AD by modulating immune signaling, reinforcing barrier function, and counteracting oxidative stress.

, a traditional East Asian medicinal plant, contains structurally diverse bioactives including lignans, neolignans, flavonoids, and butenolides. Aqueous extracts ofhave been reported to alleviate AD-like symptoms in mouse models induced by house dust mite and 2,4-dinitrochlorobenzene, decreasing ear swelling, mast-cell infiltration, and serum immunoglobulin E (IgE) levels. In addition, specific phenolic glycosides and lignans isolated from this plant suppress histamine release and pro-inflammatory cytokines in immune cells, supporting anti-allergic potential. While previous studies primarily focused on crude water or ethanol extracts, the mechanistic basis of’s anti-atopic effects remains unclear. Previous studies mainly assessed crude extracts at the organismal level without distinguishing epithelial and immune contributions, leaving the specific cellular targets and active chemical classes undefined. Systematic fractionation provides a means to isolate chemically distinct constituents and to delineate which bioactive class drives epithelial and immune modulation. Therefore, this study aimed to identify an active solvent-partitioned fraction ofand to characterize its effects on keratinocyte-centered inflammatory mediator production and MC903-induced AD-like skin inflammation.

Notably, among solvent-partitioned fractions of, the methylene chloride layeris enriched in lipophilic constituents that may modulate barrier-related processes and intracellular signaling differently from polar fractions. Fraction-based screening in cytokine-stimulated HaCaT keratinocytes, using IL-8 and TSLP suppression as selection markers, identified LM as the most active layer, which was subsequently chosen for detailed evaluation. We then evaluated the cutaneous anti-inflammatory activity of LM through complementary in vitro and in vivo approaches addressing keratinocyte inflammatory responses, epithelial–monocytic communication, and tissue-level AD-like pathology. Solvent-fraction screening was conducted in cytokine-stimulated HaCaT keratinocytes to identify active fractions based on IL-8 and TSLP suppression. Topical LM was then tested in an MC903-induced mouse model of AD to assess clinical, histological, and molecular outcomes in skin and draining lymph nodes. Exploratory immune-cell profiling was performed to assess CD4 + T cell transcription factor-associated profiles in the draining lymph-node compartment, and a HaCaT–THP-1 co-culture system was employed to assess epithelial–monocytic inflammatory communication.

The present study was designed to determine whether LM suppresses keratinocyte-derived inflammatory mediators under distinct cytokine- and innate immune-stimulatory conditions and attenuates MC903-induced AD-like skin inflammation in vivo. Using complementary keratinocyte monoculture, HaCaT–THP-1 co-culture, and MC903-induced mouse models, this study evaluated the anti-inflammatory activity of LM at epithelial, epithelial–monocytic, and tissue levels, while recognizing that these models do not establish a direct cell-specific mechanism linking keratinocyte responses to lymph-node immune-cell profiles.

Results

Anti-inflammatory screening of solvent fractions of

To identify a lead anti-inflammatory fraction, HaCaT keratinocytes were treated with five solvent fractions (-hexane, methylene chloride, ethyl acetate,-butanol, and water) derived from the methanol extract of, each tested at the same concentration (20 µg/mL). The methylene chloride fraction, which was selected for subsequent analyses, is hereafter referred to as LM. At the screening concentration, none of the tested fractions exhibited detectable cytotoxicity in HaCaT keratinocytes under either IFN-γ/TNF-α or poly(I: C) (polyinosine-polycytidylic acid) stimulation (Fig.a and c). Under these non-cytotoxic conditions, IFN-γ and TNF-α stimulation significantly increased IL-8 secretion compared with the CON group, whereas all fractions reduced IL-8 levels relative to the stimulated group. Among them, LM showed a significantly greater inhibitory effect than the parent methanol extract (MeOH) (= 0.0040 vs. MeOH, Fig.b). Likewise, poly(I: C) markedly increased TSLP secretion, and LM also significantly reduced TSLP levels compared with the parent methanol extract (= 0.0354 vs. MeOH, Fig.d). Based on these screening results, LM was selected as the lead fraction for subsequent phytochemical characterization.

Therefore, whereas TPC reflects the overall abundance of phenolic compounds, the chromatographic and spectroscopic data more specifically support the interpretation that LM is relatively enriched in lignan-type metabolites.

LM alleviates AD-like skin lesions in MC903-induced AD mouse model

Topical administration of LM alleviated AD-like symptoms in an MC903-induced mouse model. The experimental protocol is shown in Fig.a. The LM efficacy outcomes shown in Fig.were obtained from the complete CON/AD/LM efficacy experiment, which included CON, AD, and LM groups. Before this efficacy experiment, two independent CON/AD model-establishment experiments were performed to confirm the induction of MC903-induced AD-like phenotypes. These preliminary experiments included only CON and AD groups and did not include LM treatment. Progressive ear thickening was observed in MC903-treated mice in both preliminary experiments, while body weight was monitored in parallel. The preliminary CON/AD data are provided in Supplementary Fig. S2 for transparency and were not pooled with the LM efficacy analysis. Body weight was significantly decreased in the AD group compared with the CON group, whereas LM treatment prevented weight loss and partially restored body weight (day 14;= 0.0246, Fig.b). Representative gross ear images and histological analyses showed that MC903-induced AD caused marked erythema, scaling, and epidermal hyperplasia, increased epidermal thickness, and inflammatory-cell infiltration, all of which were attenuated by LM treatment (Fig.c). Consistent with these observations, ear thickness progressively increased in the AD group but was significantly reduced in the LM group (day 14;< 0.0001, Fig.d). Furthermore, AD induction markedly increased spleen weight compared with the CON group, indicating systemic immune activation, whereas LM treatment significantly suppressed spleen enlargement (< 0.0001, Fig.e). Similarly, serum IgE levels were reduced in the LM-treated group compared with the AD group (= 0.0181, Fig.f), although they remained elevated relative to the CON group. Collectively, these findings indicate that LM ameliorated several local and systemic features of MC903-induced AD-like inflammation.

Conservative exploratory flow-cytometric reanalysis suggested LM-associated changes in CD4 + T-cell transcription factor profiles in cervical lymph nodes

In addition to local skin evaluation by ear histology and ear-tissue cytokine gene expression, exploratory immune-cell profiling was performed in cervical lymph nodes to assess changes in the draining immune compartment. Flow-cytometric analysis was performed using a sequential gating strategy. Lymphocyte populations were first identified based on FSC-A and SSC-A characteristics, followed by FSC-A/FSC-H singlet discrimination and viability gating. Because fluorescence-minus-one (FMO) controls were not included and transcription factor signals showed partially continuous distributions, these data were not interpreted as discrete helper T cell subsets. Instead, the analysis was limited to conservatively gated transcription factor-associated CD4 + T cell profiles. For GATA3 and T-bet analysis, positive thresholds were placed more conservatively to minimize inclusion of cells with intermediate or continuous staining signals, and the same thresholds were applied consistently across all samples and groups.

The overall proportion of CD3 + CD4+ cells among live singlet cells did not differ significantly among the CON, AD, and LM groups (Fig.A). Within CD3 + CD4+ cells, the conservatively gated GATA3-T-bet+ profile was significantly reduced in the AD group compared with the CON group, whereas LM treatment showed only a modest, non-significant increase relative to the AD group (Fig.B). Conversely, the conservatively gated GATA3 + T-bet- profile was significantly increased in the AD group compared with the CON group, and LM treatment significantly reduced this profile compared with the AD group (Fig.B). Total RORγt+CD4 + T cell-associated profiles, including co-expressing cells, did not differ significantly among the groups (Fig.C). In contrast, total Foxp3 + CD4+ T cell-associated profiles, including co-expressing cells, were increased in the AD group compared with the CON group and showed a decreasing tendency in the LM-treated group (Fig.C). Together, these exploratory findings suggest that LM treatment was associated with changes in selected CD4 + T cell transcription factor-associated profiles in the cervical lymph nodes, particularly a reduction in the AD-associated increase in the GATA3 + T-bet- profile, but they do not establish direct effects on helper T cell differentiation or function. Because the CD49b/Siglec-F panel did not include sufficient lineage-defining markers to identify granulocyte or eosinophil subsets, these data were not included in the main mechanistic interpretation and are provided only as exploratory CD49b/Siglec-F marker-defined CD45 + cell expression profiles in Supplementary Fig. S4.

Discussion

AD is classically recognized as a type 2-skewed inflammatory skin disease; however, its pathophysiology involves broader epidermal–immune inflammatory networks, including keratinocyte activation, innate immune signaling, and cytokine-mediated amplification across multiple immune pathways. Transcriptomic studies of acute and chronic AD lesions further support this view by showing overlapping Th2, Th1, Th17, IL-36, and keratinocyte-associated inflammatory signatures during disease progression. In particular, keratinocytes respond not only to Th2-associated mediators but also to pro-inflammatory cytokine combinations such as IFN-γ and TNF-α, thereby inducing chemokines and inflammatory mediators involved in leukocyte recruitment and epidermal immune activation. Previous HaCaT-based optimization studies also showed that AD-related gene-expression patterns are best reproduced by a mixed cytokine milieu involving innate, Th1-, and Th2-associated signals, including IFN-γ and TNF-α. Therefore, anti-inflammatory agents that suppress keratinocyte-derived mediators across different inflammatory contexts may have broader relevance for AD-associated and cytokine-mediated cutaneous inflammation.

In the present study, bioactivity-guided fractionation identified the methylene chloride fraction fromas a lead anti-inflammatory fraction. Phytochemical profiling by HPLC-UV and ¹H NMR revealed that LM is a lignan-enriched fraction, as supported by spectral features consistent with enrichment of moderately polar, non-glycosidic metabolites. Lignans represent a structurally diverse class of phenylpropanoid dimers with considerable variation in oxidation state, substitution pattern, and stereochemistry, which may complicate separation of individual constituents. Nevertheless, the integrated chromatographic and spectroscopic analyses clearly supported the characterization of LM as a lignan-enriched fraction. This is noteworthy because previous studies onhave mainly emphasized anti-inflammatory effects of phenolic glycosides and secobutanolides, including suppression of histamine release and cytokine production in mast cells and inhibition of IL-6 and IL-12 p40 in dendritic cells. Our findings broaden this pharmacological profile by suggesting that a lignan-enriched fraction can also modulate epithelial inflammatory responses relevant to AD. The in vitro systems used here were intended to model keratinocyte-centered inflammatory activation and epithelial innate inflammatory responses rather than to fully reproduce the Th2-dominant immune milieu of AD. In particular, poly(I: C)-induced keratinocyte activation is relevant to epithelial innate inflammatory responses because double-stranded RNA stimulation can induce TSLP and other inflammatory mediators in human keratinocytes in a cytokine milieu-dependent manner.

The biological relevance of LM was further supported in the MC903-induced mouse model of AD, which reproduces key features of human disease, including epidermal hyperplasia, immune cell infiltration, elevated serum IgE, and Th2-skewed immune activation. Topical LM treatment alleviated visible skin lesions and reduced ear thickness, epidermal hyperplasia, spleen enlargement, and serum IgE, supporting both local and systemic anti-inflammatory effects. At the tissue level, LM was associated with reduced expression of,,, andin ear tissues, together with decreased,,,, andexpression in the draining cervical lymph nodes. Rather than indicating action on a single downstream effector pathway, this overall pattern is more consistent with modulation of the skin–lymph node inflammatory axis that links epithelial activation to regional immune responses in AD. In particular, suppression of TSLP and CXCL1 in skin is notable because these mediators contribute to epidermal activation and leukocyte recruitment, whereas reduced IL-4, IL-6, and IL-33 expression in cervical lymph nodes suggests attenuation of downstream cytokine amplification within the regional immune compartment.

These observations are broadly consistent with previous reports thatcontains multiple classes of anti-inflammatory metabolites capable of modulating inflammatory signaling. Phenolic glycosides isolated fromsuppressed mast cell-derived TNF-α and IL-6 and inhibited histamine release, while Haque et al. demonstrated that compounds from this plant can attenuate inflammatory signaling by inhibiting NF-κB and mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) activation. Although those studies focused primarily on glycosides rather than lignans, they support the view that anti-inflammatory activity ofis not restricted to a single constituent class. In this regard, the present findings suggest that lignan-enriched fractions may also contribute to the therapeutic potential of this plant in AD-related inflammation.

The flow-cytometric data should be interpreted as exploratory supportive observations rather than definitive evidence of helper T cell subset differentiation. FSC-A/FSC-H singlet discrimination was included after lymphocyte gating and before viability gating to minimize the potential influence of doublets. In addition, because FMO controls were not available, the available flow-cytometric data were reanalyzed using a more conservative gating strategy for GATA3 and T-bet. Positive thresholds were placed to reduce inclusion of cells with intermediate or continuous staining signals, and the same thresholds were applied consistently across all samples and groups. Although the absence of FMO controls remains a limitation, the representative GATA3/T-bet plots showed that GATA3 + T-bet- CD4 + T cells were almost undetectable in CON samples but clearly emerged in AD samples. The positive gate was placed conservatively, toward the high-intensity region, to minimize inclusion of intermediate staining signals; therefore, this approach may underestimate rather than overestimate the frequency of GATA3 + cells. However, because FMO controls were not included and transcription factor expression showed partially continuous distributions, quadrant-defined populations were not used to make strong claims regarding discrete Th1, Th2, Th17, or regulatory T cell subsets. Instead, the analysis focused on conservatively gated CD4 + T cell transcription factor-associated profiles in the draining cervical lymph nodes, which represent a regional immune compartment associated with MC903-induced skin inflammation. The AD group showed a reduced GATA3-T-bet+ profile and an increased GATA3 + T-bet- profile compared with the CON group. LM treatment significantly reduced the GATA3 + T-bet- profile, whereas the GATA3-T-bet+ profile showed only a modest, non-significant increase relative to the AD group. The marked AD-associated increase in the GATA3 + T-bet- profile, together with its reduction by LM treatment, supports an LM-associated modulation of Th2-associated, or GATA3-associated, CD4 + T-cell profiles in the draining cervical lymph nodes. These findings should be interpreted in the context of the complex and dynamic inflammatory environment of AD-like skin inflammation, in which multiple immune axes may contribute to disease progression and resolution. Total RORγt+CD4 + T cell-associated profiles, including co-expressing cells, did not differ significantly among the groups, whereas total Foxp3 + CD4+ T cell-associated profiles, including co-expressing cells, were increased in the AD group and showed a decreasing tendency in the LM-treated group. The functional significance of the Foxp3-associated change remains unclear because suppressive function was not assessed, and Foxp3 expression alone does not establish regulatory T cell function. Therefore, these data suggest that LM treatment may be associated with altered immune profiles in the regional lymph-node compartment, particularly by reducing the AD-associated increase in the GATA3 + T-bet- transcription factor-associated profiles, but they do not establish direct effects on helper T cell differentiation or function. This cautious interpretation is consistent with flow-cytometry best-practice recommendations emphasizing the importance of appropriate controls and careful positivity assignment, particularly when fluorescence distributions are not clearly separated.

The CD49b/Siglec-F analysis was removed from the main mechanistic interpretation because the antibody panel did not include sufficient lineage-defining markers. Accordingly, CD49b and Siglec-F expression among CD45 + cervical lymph node cells is presented only as exploratory supplementary profiling and is not used to draw conclusions regarding granulocyte or eosinophil subsets. These data should therefore be described as CD49b/Siglec-F marker-defined CD45 + cell expression profiles rather than as eosinophil or granulocyte subset identification. Importantly, local cutaneous responses in this study were assessed by histological examination and cytokine gene-expression analysis in ear tissue, whereas immune-cell profiling was performed in cervical lymph nodes. Therefore, these flow-cytometric findings should be interpreted as reflecting LM-associated modulation in the draining lymph-node compartment rather than direct evidence of immune-cell infiltration within lesional skin.

The co-culture findings suggest that LM may influence epithelial–monocytic inflammatory communication. In THP-1 monocytes co-cultured with IFN-γ- and TNF-α-stimulated HaCaT keratinocytes, LM reduced the expression of,,, and. Because these mediators are involved in leukocyte recruitment and inflammatory signaling, their simultaneous downregulation is consistent with reduced inflammatory activity in the co-culture setting. However, this model was used to assess inflammatory epithelial–monocytic communication in a distinct in vitro setting and should not be interpreted as a direct mechanistic surrogate for the lymph-node immune-profile changes observed in vivo. Therefore, the co-culture findings support reduced inflammatory activity under the defined epithelial–monocytic co-culture conditions used here. Because direct LM treatment of THP-1 monocultures was not included in the present study, the current co-culture data do not allow definitive determination of whether LM directly affects THP-1 responses or whether the observed changes are mediated primarily through altered keratinocyte-derived inflammatory signaling. Further studies will be needed to clarify any direct effect of LM on THP-1 cells.

A key consideration in interpreting the present study is that the in vitro and in vivo systems address different biological levels and should not be considered directly equivalent. The IFN-γ/TNF-α- and poly(I: C)-stimulated HaCaT models were used to evaluate keratinocyte-centered inflammatory activation, including IL-8 and TSLP production, rather than to reproduce Th1/Th2 polarization or lymph-node immune-cell differentiation. In contrast, the MC903-induced mouse model represents a tissue-level AD-like inflammatory response involving epidermal activation, serum IgE elevation, cytokine gene expression in skin and draining lymph nodes, and exploratory changes in selected immune-cell-associated profiles. The HaCaT–THP-1 co-culture system was used to assess epithelial–monocytic inflammatory communication and should not be interpreted as a surrogate for CD4 + T cell or eosinophil-associated responses in vivo. This interpretation is consistent with expert reviews emphasizing that no single experimental model fully recapitulates the complex pathophysiology of human AD and that each model should be interpreted according to the biological level and cell types it represents. Therefore, the present datasets should be interpreted as complementary evidence supporting the anti-inflammatory activity of LM in epidermal inflammation, but not as direct proof of a single Th1/Th2-regulatory mechanism.

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting these findings. First, the in vivo cytokine-related data were evaluated primarily at the mRNA level, and protein-level validation in ear tissue, such as ELISA or immunohistochemistry, was not performed. Therefore, the present study supports transcriptional anti-inflammatory effects of LM, but does not establish corresponding changes in tissue cytokine protein abundance. Second, although previous studies have implicated NF-κB and MAPK signaling in AD-related inflammatory responses, these pathways were not directly examined in the present study. Third, immune-cell profiling was performed in cervical lymph nodes rather than in lesional skin. Thus, although local skin responses were assessed by histology and cytokine gene-expression analysis in ear tissue, the flow cytometric data reflect changes in the draining lymph-node compartment and do not directly define immune-cell infiltration at the site of skin inflammation. Fourth, although the available flow-cytometric data were reanalyzed using FSC-A/FSC-H singlet discrimination and more conservative GATA3/T-bet thresholds, FMO controls were not included. Therefore, the transcription factor data should be interpreted only as exploratory CD4 + T-cell transcription factor-associated profiles, not as definitive helper T-cell subsets. Fifth, the in vitro systems used in this study were designed to evaluate keratinocyte-centered inflammatory activation and epithelial–monocytic communication, rather than to reproduce the full Th2-dominant immune milieu of MC903-induced AD or to define mechanisms in specific downstream immune-cell subsets. Thus, the present study supports the anti-inflammatory activity of LM across complementary experimental systems, while cell-specific immune mechanisms remain to be defined. Finally, the present study was conducted using a single prepared extract batch, and inter-batch phytochemical or biological consistency was not evaluated. LM was also characterized as a lignan-enriched fraction rather than as a purified single compound, and no specific receptor or direct molecular target was identified. Therefore, receptor-level interpretation remains limited. Future studies should identify the major active constituents of LM, evaluate their anti-inflammatory activity individually, and investigate potential receptor interactions and signaling mechanisms through molecular docking and experimental validation.

In conclusion, LM, a lignan-enriched methylene chloride fraction of, suppressed keratinocyte-derived inflammatory mediators and attenuated MC903-induced AD-like skin inflammation. The in vivo findings indicate reduced epidermal pathology, serum IgE, inflammatory cytokine gene expression, and exploratory changes in selected lymph-node immune-cell-associated profiles. These findings support LM as a promising anti-inflammatory fraction for AD-associated epidermal inflammation. Nevertheless, the present data should be interpreted without assuming a direct mechanistic link between the in vitro keratinocyte-based assays and the in vivo CD4 + T cell-associated findings, and future studies will be required to define its active constituents and cell-specific immune mechanisms.

Methods

Plant extraction

The dried stems ofwere purchased from Donguiherb (Seoul, Republic of Korea). The dried stems (21 kg) were extracted with 100% methanol (50 L each, three times) at room temperature for 24 h per extraction.

The combined methanol extracts were concentrated under reduced pressure using a rotary evaporator (N-1300, EYELA, Tokyo, Japan) to yield 1.1 kg of crude extract (5.2%, w/w). The crude extract was suspended in water and successively partitioned with n-hexane, methylene chloride, ethyl acetate, n-butanol, and water. Each solvent fraction was concentrated to dryness under reduced pressure to yield the n-hexane (65 g), methylene chloride (60 g), ethyl acetate (125 g), n-butanol (200 g), and aqueous fractions (650 g). All solvents were of analytical grade and obtained from Daejung Chemicals (Siheung, Republic of Korea).

HPLC profiling and NMR phytochemical fingerprinting

High-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) analysis was performed using Waters 515 pumps (Waters Corporation, Milford, MA, USA) equipped with a 2995 PDA detector (Waters Corporation). Chromatographic separation was carried out on a Phenomenex Gemini NX-C18 column (5 μm, 150 × 10 mm; Phenomenex, Torrance, CA, USA). The mobile phase consisted of distilled water (solvent A) and HPLC-grade methanol (solvent B). The gradient program was as follows: 10% B (0–10 min), 10–100% B (10–60 min), and 100% B (60–70 min) for washing. The flow rate was set to 2.5 mL/min, the injection volume was 20 µL, column temperature was maintained at room temperature, and UV detection was performed at 280 nm.

H nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectra were acquired on a JEOL JNM-ECA 400 MHz spectrometer (JEOL, Tokyo, Japan; NFEC-2025-04-305229) using CDOD (Cambridge Isotope Laboratories, Inc., Tewksbury, MA, USA) as the solvent. Each sample was dissolved in CDOD and transferred to 3-mm NMR tubes. All spectra were acquired at 293 K with 32 scans. The residual solvent peak of CDOD was observed at δ3.31. Monosaccharide standards (-glucose,-fructose, and sucrose) were purchased from Sigma–Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA), and their ¹H NMR spectra were used as reference standards (Supplementary Fig.). Twenty microliters of each extract and fraction, 1 mg/mL, was added to a 96-well microplate. Subsequently, 100 µL of Folin–Ciocalteu reagent, diluted 1:10 with distilled water, Sigma–Aldrich, was added to each well and allowed to react in the dark for 5 min at room temperature. Then, 100 µL of 7.5% NaCOsolution was added, and the mixture was incubated in the dark at room temperature for 60 min. The absorbance was measured at 760 nm using a microplate reader. The TPC was expressed as gallic acid equivalents using gallic acid (0.0625–0.5 mg/mL) as a standard.

Cell culture and cell viability assay

HaCaT cells were purchased from Cell Lines Service (Eppelheim, Germany) and were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM; GenDEPOT, Barker, TX, USA), containing 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco, ON, Canada) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (Gibco BRL, Grand Island, NY, USA) in a 5% COhumidified incubator at 37 °C.

LM and the solvent fractions were dissolved in DMSO, and the final vehicle concentration was adjusted to 0.1% in all groups, including the CON and stimulated control groups.

Cell viability was determined using a MTT assay. Cells were seeded at a density of 5 × 10cells/well in a 96-well plate. After 24 h, cells were treated with LM or each solvent fraction with either 5 ng/mL IFN-γ and 5 ng/mL TNF-α (PeproTech, Rocky Hill, NJ, USA) or 5 µg/mL Poly(I:C) (Merck, Darmstadt, Germany). Following 24 h of incubation, 10 µL MTT solution (5 mg/mL, Sigma–Aldrich) was added to each well and incubated for an additional 4 h. The formazan precipitate was dissolved in DMSO. The absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader (AMR-100, Allsheng, Hangzhou, China).

In vitro HaCaT-THP-1 epidermal and monocytic co-culture model

THP-1 human monocytic cells were purchased from the Korea Cell Line Bank (Seoul, Republic of Korea) and cultured in RPMI-1640 medium supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin–streptomycin. Cells were maintained at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO₂. For co-culture assays, 5 × 10⁵ THP-1 cells were plated in the lower chambers of 24-well culture plates, and HaCaT keratinocytes (0.5 × 10⁵ cells per insert) were seeded onto 0.4-µm pore size Transwell inserts (SPL Life Sciences, Gyeonggi-do, Republic of Korea). After 24 h of incubation, HaCaT cells were stimulated with IFN-γ (5 ng/mL) and TNF-α (5 ng/mL) in the presence or absence of 20 µg/mL LM. The co-culture continued for another 24 h, after which THP-1 cells from the lower chamber were collected and subjected to downstream analyses such as RT-qPCR.

Animal experiments

All animal experimental procedures of this study were approved by the Pusan National University Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (approval number: PNU-2024-0383) and were performed in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations. All methods are reported in accordance with the ARRIVE guidelines () for the reporting of animal experiments. Six-week-old C57BL/6 N male mice were obtained from Samtako (Gyeonggi-do, Republic of Korea). The animals were kept on a 12 h light/dark cycle under temperature-regulated (25 ± 2 °C), and humidity-controlled (50 ± 5%) conditions and provided with water and diet. After one week of acclimatization, the mice were randomly assigned to three groups (= 5): a non-treated control group, MC903-treated group (AD), and MC903-treated with LM group. The experimental schedule is depicted in Fig.a. To establish an MC903-induced AD model, 80 µM of MC903 in 80% ethanol was topically applied to surfaces of each ear daily for 10 consecutive days, except for the CON. In the CON, mouse ears were treated with the solvent ethanol. Four hours after the application of MC903 or ethanol, the ears of mice were topically treated with ethanol or LM 100 mg/mL. Topical treatments (ethanol, MC903, and LM) were applied to one side of the ear using precision micropipettes in 25 µL aliquots. Ear thickness was measured at the indicated time points using a digital vernier caliper (Altraco Inc., Sausalito, CA, USA). Measurements were performed by the same trained researcher using consistent pressure to minimize measurement variability; however, formal blinding was not performed. The researcher had prior experience performing ear thickness measurements in similar animal experiments. The dosage of LM used in this study was selected based on previously reported topical application dosages ofpreparations. At the end of the experiment, mice were euthanized under deep anesthesia induced by ketamine–xylazine. Blood was collected from the orbital vein and centrifuged at 3,000for 20 min, and serum was stored at −80 °C. Ears and cervical lymph nodes were collected and stored at −80 °C for further analysis. Before conducting the LM efficacy experiment, two independent preliminary model-establishment experiments were performed to confirm the induction of MC903-induced AD-like phenotypes based on longitudinal ear thickness measurements, with body weight monitored in parallel. These preliminary experiments included only the vehicle-treated control group and the MC903-induced AD group and did not include LM treatment. The complete CON/AD/LM efficacy experiment was subsequently performed to evaluate the effects of LM and included clinical, histological, serological, molecular, and flow-cytometric endpoints. All LM-treatment animal data generated in this efficacy experiment are presented in the manuscript. Because LM was not included in the preliminary model-establishment experiments, those datasets were not applicable for pooled analysis of LM efficacy.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

HaCaT cells were seeded at a density of 5 × 10cells/well in a 96-well plate under the conditions described above. The supernatants from the cell cultures were collected and stored at −80 °C for further analysis. The levels of IL-8 (R&D Systems, Minneapolis, MN, USA) and TSLP (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) in these supernatants were quantified using specific ELISA kits. Absorbance was determined at 450 nm. Mouse serum levels of IgE (BioLegend, San Diego, CA, USA) were measured using ELISA kits.

Histological analysis

Ear tissue was fixed in 10% paraformaldehyde (Biosesang, Gyeonggi-do, Republic of Korea) for 24 h, then embedded in paraffin and sectioned at 3-µm thickness using a Shandon Finesse Me microtome (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). The sections were fixed on glass slides and stained with hematoxylin-eosin (H&E). Stained ear tissues were observed under a light microscope (ECLIPSE Ts2R; Nikon, Tokyo, Japan).

RT-qPCR analysis

Total RNA from HaCaT cells and tissues of ear and lymph nodes was isolated using Trizol reagent (Invitrogen), followed by phase separation with chloroform (Biosesang, Seoul, Republic of Korea). RNA was precipitated using isopropanol (Biosesang) and washed with 75% diethylpyrocarbonate-treated ethanol prepared from diethylpyrocarbonate-treated water and ethanol (Biosesang). The purity and concentration of the extracted RNA were assessed using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer (Nano-400 A, Allsheng). For cDNA synthesis, 500 ng of total RNA was used per reaction with AccuPowerRT PreMix (Bioneer, Daejeon, Republic of Korea). The assay was performed according to the manufacturer’s instructions. RT-qPCR was conducted using a QuantStudio 1 Real-Time PCR system (Applied Biosystems, Waltham, CA, USA) under the following cycling conditions: 2 min at 50 °C, 15 min at 95 °C, followed by 40 cycles of 20 s at 95 °C, 40 s at 60 °C, and 20 s at 72 °C, with a final melting curve analysis. The primer sequences used in this study are presented in Table. The relative gene expression levels were normalized to β-actin () or glyceraldehyde 3-phosphate dehydrogenase () and quantified using the 2method.

Cell isolation of cervical lymph node

Cervical lymph nodes were immersed in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and mechanically disaggregated. The resulting cell suspension was passed through a 40-µm strainer to remove residual tissue fragments. Cells were collected by centrifugation (400, 5 min, 4 °C), washed with PBS, and resuspended for flow cytometric analysis.

Flow cytometry analysis

For flow-cytometric analysis, 100,000 events were acquired for CD4 + T-cell transcription factor analysis, and 10,000 events were acquired for exploratory CD49b/Siglec-F CD45 + cell profiling. Prior to the main multicolor staining experiments, preliminary single-staining experiments were performed to confirm individual marker signals. In the final analysis, samples were stained with the full antibody panel, and the same gating strategy was applied consistently across all groups. Fluorescence minus one (FMO) controls and separate control antibodies were not included in the final experimental design. Single-cell suspensions from cervical lymph nodes were measured using a FACS Canto II (BD Biosciences, Heidelberg, Germany) and analyzed using FlowJo software v10.7.1 (Tree Star Inc., Ashland, OR, USA). Dead cells were excluded using LIVE/DEAD™ Fixable Aqua viability dye (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Doublets were excluded by FSC-A versus FSC-H singlet discrimination after lymphocyte gating and before viability gating. For CD4 + T cell transcription factor analysis, the gating sequence was lymphocytes → singlets → live cells → CD3+ (145-2C11, BioLegend) CD4+ (RM4-5, BioLegend) cells, followed by analysis of GATA3 (16E10A23, BioLegend), T-bet (4B10, Invitrogen), RORγt (B2D, Invitrogen), and Foxp3 (FJK-16s, Invitrogen) expression. For GATA3 and T-bet analysis, positive thresholds were placed conservatively to minimize inclusion of cells with intermediate or continuous transcription factor staining signals. The same thresholds were applied consistently across all samples and groups. For exploratory CD49b/Siglec-F CD45 + cell profiling, the gating sequence was leukocyte-like cells → singlets → live cells → CD45 + cells (30-F11, BioLegend), followed by analysis of CD49b (HMα2, BioLegend) and Siglec-F (S17007L, BioLegend) expression. Because FMO controls were not included and the CD49b/Siglec-F panel did not include sufficient lineage-defining markers, flow-cytometric data were interpreted as exploratory immune-cell profiles rather than definitive discrete immune-cell subsets. The CD4 + T cell gating strategy is provided in Supplementary Fig. S3, and exploratory CD49b/Siglec-F CD45 + cell profiling is provided in Supplementary Fig. S4.

Statistical analysis

Data were presented as means ± standard deviations (SD) and analyzed using SAS software (version 9.4; SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA). One-way Analysis of Variance (ANOVA) with Dunnett’s multiple comparison test was used to determine significant differences (< 0.05). For the preliminary CON/AD model-establishment experiments shown in Supplementary Fig. S2, comparisons between CON and AD groups at each time point were performed using an unpaired two-tailed Student’s t-test. These preliminary experiments were analyzed only to document MC903 model establishment and were not included in the statistical analysis of LM efficacy.
