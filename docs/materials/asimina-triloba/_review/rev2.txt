INTRODUCTION

(North American pawpaw) is unfamiliar to many and botanically unusual. It should not to be confused with papaya (), also known as pawpaw in some parts of the world.is a tree fruit from northernmost genus and only temperate member in the tropical Annonaceae family of commercially viable fruits such as(cherimoya),(sugar apple),(soursop),(custard apple), and others. It is the largest edible fruit native to North America.fruit is botanically categorized as a pulpy berry.Only the fruit pulp, not the skin or seeds are consumed.

Theindustry is niche but expanding.fruit harvest occurs once a year from mid‐summer to mid‐fall in the temperate regions of the US Department of Agriculture (USDA) Hardiness Zones 5–9. A recent review,internet searches, and personal communications indicatedis introduced in at least 18 European and four Asian countries. The typicalgrower cultivates a small number of trees with annual production of a few hundred pounds,although there are larger operations, Most sell fruit directly to consumers or fresh or frozen processed pulp, usually with the generic label ‘pawpaw’ rather than with a cultivar name, to the wholesale market for beer, ice cream, and other products.

General accounts ofhave existed for centuries, but the first written record ofcultivar development dates to the 1890swhen ‘Uncle Tom’ was described as the first namedcultivar. Particularly notable was a 1916 competition sponsored by the American Genetics Association called ‘Where are the Best Papaws’ with the winners announced in 1917.,The winning fruit, known as cultivar ‘Ketter’, the other finalists, and many early cultivars, are lost to history.The 1980s brought renewed commercialization efforts and a proliferation of named cultivars, some of which are patented and the names trademarked.

A systematic search revealed only a few hundred papers that represent the totality ofliterature from 1900, with most of the focus on preharvest growth characteristics, however, many do not identify the cultivars that were assayed. Specifically, the analysis identified only 22 peer‐reviewed papers meeting the inclusion criteria. This low number is a direct and empirical indicator of the current research landscape, underscoring the significant knowledge gaps that this review aims to consolidate and highlight. The purpose of this review was to present an overview of postharvest biochemical, biophysical and biological properties of named cultivars of. Descriptive statistics were calculated and reported for attributes from the same cultivar across multiple research studies, and correlations also were calculated between some attributes across cultivars. No attempt was made to describe the data across studies using techniques such as meta‐analysis because the low number of papers would require assumptions about effect size, heterogeneity, and other factors, which would call into question their validity. The scope of this analysis was further delimited to exclude two topics that could be considered postharvest quality attributes of pawpaw: the detailed sensory characteristics of individual named cultivars,and the cytotoxicity and health benefits of annonaceous acetogenins.,,

CHARACTERISTICS OF THE STUDIES THAT DESCRIBECULTIVAR DIFFERENCES

This review identified 22 studies in which the fruits from 61 individualcultivars were analyzed for biochemical, biological, and biophysical properties. The studies analyzed between one and nine bio‐properties, with an average (mean) of 5.5, a median of 6, and a mode of 7 per study. Fruit weight was reported for all 61 named cultivars. Five fruit attributes (°Brix, pH, pulp color, skin color, texture) were reported for 27 or more named cultivars. Six fruit attributes (antioxidant capacity, dimensions, enzymes, moisture, total flavonoids, total phenolics) were reported for 15 to 24 of the cultivars, and the remaining 12 attributes were reported for 11 or fewer cultivars. No individual cultivar was analyzed for all 24 bio‐properties and the majority of the 61 cultivars were analyzed for fewer than ten.

It is worth noting that this analysis identified certain treatment factors common in the literature for other fruits, such as ripeness level, effect of processing, ingredient addition, packaging, and storage conditions. This review attempts to incorporate these treatment factors into the discussion for certain bio‐properties because it would be beneficial to compare named cultivars across these factors from a postharvest perspective. However, the reader is cautioned that the lack of literature prevents robust comparisons for all bio‐properties.

BIOCHEMICAL PROPERTIES OFFRUIT

Antioxidative properties

The antioxidative properties ofinclude the amount and type of phytochemicals and their antioxidant capacity. Phytochemicals are a broad category of naturally occurring compounds such as polyphenols, carotenoids, and vitamins, that contribute to the color of fruits and also have antioxidant properties.methods are often employed to evaluate the efficacy of phytochemicals. These methods often are referred to as antioxidant capacity assays. The number of named cultivars for which data on antioxidative properties was obtained ranged from 2 to 21.

Total phenolics and total flavonoids

Total phenolic compounds were reported for 21 individualcultivars across seven studies (Table). Total phenolics were quantified using two different polyphenol standards; five studies used gallic acid as the standard and reported total phenolics for 18 cultivars, and two studies used chlorogenic acid as the standard and reported total phenolics for nine cultivars. The values of the different systems are not directly comparable, but relative comparisons are possible. The range of total phenolics for the 18 cultivars that used gallic acid as a standard was 0.6–4.6 mg gallic acid equivalents g(mean () = 2.1, standard deviation (SD) = 1.4). The range of total phenolics for the eight cultivars that used chlorogenic acid as a standard was 25–74 mg chlorogenic acid equivalents 100 g(= 51, SD = 14). Five cultivars intersect between these two methods, with good agreement for three. Cultivars ‘Wilson’, ‘Taylor’ were high and middling in total phenolics, respectively, compared to the other cultivars regardless of the standard, and ‘KSU‐Atwood’ and ‘Taytwo’ were low in total phenolics compared to the other cultivars regardless of the standard used. However, ‘PA‐Golden’ exhibited a high total phenolic value when chlorogenic acid was used as a standard but low when gallic acid was used compared to the other cultivars.

Total flavonoids were reported for 16 individualcultivars from three manuscripts (Table). Total flavonoids were quantified using two different flavonoid standards; two studies reporting total flavonoids for 11 cultivars used rutin as the standard, whereas one study reporting total flavonoids for six cultivars used catechin as the standard. The values of the different systems are not directly comparable, but relative comparisons are possible. The range of total flavonoids for the 11 cultivars that used rutin as a standard was 0.04–0.86 mmol rutin equivalents g(= 0.24, SD = 0.28). The range of total flavonoids for the six cultivars that used catechin as a standard was 10–33 mg catechin equivalents 100 g(= 51, SD = 14). A single cultivar intersects between these two methods, with good agreement. Cultivar ‘NC‐1’ was low in total flavonoids compared to the other cultivars regardless of the standard used.

The methods used for total phenolics and flavonoids produced a wide range of values across the studies. A Pearson correlation coefficient was computed to assess the linear relationship for the ten cultivars in common between total phenolics and total flavonoids. No correlation between total phenolics and total flavonoids was observed ((8) = 0.152,= 0.674).

Several studies examined the change in total phenolics and flavonoids during ripening. The total phenolic content of ‘PA Golden’ tended to decrease during ripeningalthough this was not observed in ‘Davis’or in wild fruit.However, Harris and Brannan reported that wild pulp from ripefruit contained statistically fewer flavonoids than either underripe or overripe fruit.Because ripening levels at harvest are subjective, Brannan. speculate that differences they observed in total phenolics and flavonoids between species could be due to slight differences in ripeness levels and for this reason they caution against concluding that cultivars such as ‘Rebecca's Gold’ or ‘SAA Zimmerman’, which exhibited statistically higher flavonoids in their study, are superior to other cultivars.In the six cultivars grown in Italy, the pooled total phenolic and flavonoid values were positively correlated across growing years that had the highest rainfall during fruit ripening.

It is also important to acknowledge methodological concerns with respect to total phenolics and flavonoids. The methods used to produce these values have been criticized because they generate broad, non‐specific responses across the entire category of phenolics and flavonoids, and the conditions of the methods can cause reactions with a range of substances, not just phenolic compounds.,

Polyphenolic compounds

Two studies report the identification of individual phytochemical compounds. The phytochemical composition has been reported for cultivar ‘Davis’ by Donno.and for ten cultivars by Brannan.The phytochemicals quantified using authentic standards in cultivar ‘Davis’ were, in order of predominance, epicatechin, catechin, quercitrin, quercetin, ferulic acid, ellagic acid, coumaric acid, isoquercitrin, gallic acid, caffeic acid, chlorogenic acid, although these compounds were not positively identified and confirmed by mass spectrometry. Of these, only epicatechin was found in the ten cultivars assayed by Brannan., which employed mass spectrometry to positively identify and confirm the compounds. Other compounds identified in Brannan. were dimers and trimers of epicatechin (i.e., procyanadins), protocatechuic acid hexoside,‐coumaroyl hexoside, and 5‐‐‐coumaroylquinic acid.This study also detected five compounds that were not able to be identified, including what probably are procyanidin polymers with more than three epicatechin units.

Carotenoids

Carotenoids can be reported as individual compounds or total carotenoids. Quantification of total carotenoids was performed in a single studyon eight named cultivars. The amount of carotenoids ranged from 1.5 to 6.1 μg total carotenoids per g (= 3.9, SD = 1.7), in the order (from least to most) of ‘KSU Atwood’, ‘Shenandoah’, ‘Wells’, ‘Middletown’, ‘Taytwo’, ‘PA Golden’, ‘Taylor’, and ‘Wilson’. Nam. reported 82 μg 100 g‐carotene pulp from unnamedgrown in Korea.

Ascorbic acid and glutathione

Quantification of ascorbic acid and glutathione was performed in two studies, one on cultivar ‘Taytwo’and the other on cultivar ‘Davis’.Galli.reported reduced ascorbic acid levels inpulp that probably convert to 4.4 mg 100 gascorbic acid. Donnoreports ascorbic acid levels of 7.3 mg 100 gascorbic acid. These values align with values of 1.0 mg 100 gand 7.6 mg 100 gthat were measured forpulp from two unnamed cultivars. It should be noted that a previous studyreported an ascorbic acid value that was much higher (30.5 mg 100 g) because this study analyzed both the pulp and the inedible skin, likely inflating the value.

Galli.noted that refrigerated storage of whole fruit for up to 8 weeks did not change the relationship between reduced and oxidized forms of ascorbic acid and glutathione, but the total amount of each declined. This result, coupled with the observation to be reported in more detail in Sectionthat ascorbate peroxidase and glutathione reductase exhibited inconclusive responses to cold storage, led these authors to speculate that ascorbate–glutathione cycle indoes not contribute to antioxidant protection offruit at extended periods of cold storage.

Antioxidant capacity

The antioxidant capacity ofwas reported for 19 individual cultivars (Table) from five manuscripts.,,,,Specifically, these papers report five antioxidant capacity measurements. During the period when these assays were performed (2007–2021), criticisms of the utility of these assays from a technical and conceptual perspective were taking hold in the literature.,,,Of the factors that contribute to the lack of credibility of antioxidant capacity measurements, probably the most important is the lack of standardized operating procedures. FRAP (ferric ion reducing antioxidant power) has been recommended compared to othersand ORAC (oxygen radical absorbance capacity) was highlighted as an acceptable assay provided very specific conditions were met.However, this review reports the existence of the antioxidant capacity measurements for the 19 individual cultivars, shown in Table, without providing any context or comparison. Readers are encouraged to visit the original research sources to make their own interpretations.

An exception is the study that tested the antioxidative potential of extracts from nine varieties ofpulp on a turkey model system.This study reported thatpulp extracts from all nine varieties, ‘Atwood’, ‘Green River Belle’, ‘Lynn's Favorite’, ‘NC‐1’, ‘Overleese’, ‘Quaker's Delight’, ‘Rebecca's Gold’, ‘SAA Zimmerman’, and ‘Taytwo’, were as effective as grape seed extract in inhibiting markers of lipid oxidation in turkey muscle homogenate compared to the untreated control. The authors suggested that value could be added toas a potential food source of functional natural antioxidants for use in meat products, although this suggestion remains unexplored in the literature.

Pulp composition

In this review, certain chemical components offruit pulp are classified as biochemical properties because they play roles in various biological and metabolic functions. Because much of the literature on the components ofpulp was performed on composites of cultivars or on fruit to which the cultivar was not attributed, some of these studies are referenced in this section for context.

Soluble solids content ()

The °Brix, used as a measure of percent sugar, was reported for fruit from 29 individualcultivars (Table). The °Brix values for the 29 individual cultivars ranged from 13.9% to 28.0% (= 20.4%, SD = 3.8). It is interesting to note that the five studies performed at Ohio University,,,,reported higher °Brix ranges across the 21 cultivars (20.8–24.3%) compared to the range (15.2–18.6%) of four studies performed at other locations,,,reporting °Brix for 13 cultivars. Ripening level ofat harvest remains subjective, so systematic harvesting of ‘more ripe’ fruit could explain this difference because riper fruit would be expected to contain a higher sugar content.

Recent research on unnamed cultivars,indicates that the sugar content can be accounted for by three sugars: two monosaccharides (glucose and fructose) and a disaccharide (sucrose). The level of glucose (= 0.3 g 100 g) and sucrose (= 2.0 g 100 g) reported at harvest for ‘Taytwo’were much lower than the values reported in either Brannan.for glucose (= 2.7 g 100 g) and sucrose (= 11.4 g 100 g) or Nam.for glucose (= 2.1 g 100 g) or sucrose (= 9.3 g 100 g).

Complex carbohydrates (starch and fiber)

Quantification of starch was performed in a single studyon cultivar ‘Taytwo’. These authors observed a decline in starch content from a peak in unripe fruit through the ripening process and also during refrigerated storage. Recent research on unnamed or pooled cultivars indicates that dietary fiber content ofis in the range of 4.5%to 5.8%.Identification of the individual fiber composition ofpulp was performed by glycome profiling of the cultivars ‘Greenriver Belle’ and ‘Susquehanna’ and indicated the presence of both pectins and hemicelluloses.The pectins indicated in the cultivars were a homogalacturonan backbone and rhamnogalacturonans I and II. Hemicelluloses indicated in the cultivars were arabinogalactan I–IV, glucomannan, fucosylated and non‐fucosylated xyloglucans, and xylans. Although glycome profiling can be considered a quantitative method, the work onreported relative abundance rather than actual concentrations.

Water content

Water content was reported for ten cultivarsand dry matter was reported for six cultivars in a single study,although the reporting of ‘dry matter’ in that study was an inadvertent labeling error confirmed by personal communication with corresponding author, so figure 2 of Lolletti. actually reports the moisture content.The water content for the 15 cultivars, one of which was in common between the two studies, is shown in Table. The percentage across the 15 cultivars ranged from 71.1–78.0% (= 74.2%, SD = 1.9) which are in good agreement with previous research from Korea (= 79.1%) from unnamed cultivarsand Ohio, USA (= 74.5%) performed on a composite of 16 cultivars.

Organic acids and titratable acidity

There are no reports of organic acid composition in named cultivars of pawpaw, however, two papers report the concentration of organic acids in unnamed or pooled pawpaw pulp, one for fruit grown in Georgia, USAand the other for pawpaws grown in Korea.Malic, citric, succinic, oxalic, acetic, formic, and tartaric acids were reported in one or both of the studies. Of the four organic acids assayed in the fruit grown in Georgia, USA, the order of predominance was malic acid (322 mg 100 g), citric acid (16 mg 100 g), and succinic acid (5 mg 100 g), and oxalic acid was not detected. The Korean researchers assayed for seven acids and the order of predominance was acetic acid (62 mg 100 g), formic acid (43 mg 100 g), oxalic acid (37 mg 100 g), malic acid (31 mg 100 g), and citric acid (9 mg 100 g). Succinic and tartaric acids were not detected. Titratable acidity is reported for two named varieties, variety ‘Taytwo’ (0.94 mmol malic acid equivalents 100 g)and variety ‘Allegheny’ (0.75 mmol malic acid equivalents 100 g).

The pH

Data for pH of the fruit pulp is shown in Table. The pH values across the 24 cultivars ranged from 5.5–6.8 (= 6.1, SD = 0.3). These values are lower than the pH 6.9 reported as the ‘natural pH of pawpaw fruit’ pulp initially reported in 2007, measured as a composite across five cultivars.In fact, the current review only identified one of the 27 cultivars tested across the six studies (‘Rebecca's Gold’) which had a mean pH above 6.5.

Weight of wholefruits

Shown in Table, average fruit weights were reported for 61 cultivars and the average fruit weights reported in the nine studies for each cultivar ranged from 10 g to 326 g (= 129, SD = 69). It is challenging to make generalizable conclusions based on this data because fruit weight incan be influenced by many preharvest factors, including pollination,climate,and active orchard management procedures,to name a few. Of the 61 cultivars reported herein, the 12 lightest and 18 of the lightest 20 were grown by a single farmer in southwest Ohio,raising the question of whether these cultivars do not produce large fruit or whether these cultivars simply were not suited to the conditions of the growing location. Conversely, the three cultivars selected after many years of research at Kentucky State University (‘KSU‐Atwood’, ‘KSU‐Benson’, ‘KSU‐Chappell’) were ranked in the heaviest quartile and were the seventh, first, and second heaviest cultivars, respectively. The importance of efforts such as the regional variety trials, for which multiple cultivars ofwere grown in sites across the United States, that were initiated in the 1990s but largely abandoned outside of Kentucky in later years again could be recognized as valuable.

Fruit weights from 39 namedcultivars that were sorted into five groups according to their genetic connectionwere analyzed. Greenawalt.identified the two statistically heaviest genetic groups, labeled as ‘Susquehanna’ and ‘Overleese’ because the genetic groupings were related to these cultivars, but cautioned against using the data to connect genotypic similarity to phenotypic traits. Limited similarity was observed in the current review because half of the cultivars in group ‘Susquehanna’ rank in the fourth quartile (the heaviest) and the other half in the second quartile, whereas eight of the nine cultivars in group ‘Overleese’ are in the third or fourth quartile of fruit weight.

Size (length and height)

fruit shapes are imperfectly spheroidal, ovoid, or amygdaloid (almond‐shaped) and some exhibit a characteristic ‘waist’ or are slightly curved like a banana. Regardless of the specific shape, three linear dimensions are measurable on most fruit. Fruit dimensions were reported for 25 named cultivars offrom five article (Table), and most report the two longest dimensions, hereafter referred to as the length and height.lengths ranged from 7 to 16 cm (= 12, SD = 3) and heights ranged from 5 cm to 11 cm (= 7, SD = 2). The ratio of length to height across thecultivars ranged from 1.4 to 2.3 (= 1.7, SD = 0.2). A Pearson correlation coefficient was computed to assess the linear relationship betweenlength and height. Skin and pulp color

Skin color

CIELab color values were reported for 27 and 29 named cultivars ofskin (Table) and pulp (Table), respectively. CIELab color values for lightness (*), red to green (*), and blue to yellow (*) were reported. Hue angle (arctan of*/*) and other calculated values such as browning index, chroma, and total color difference have been reported.,

skin* values across the 27 cultivars ranged from 34.0 to 69.8 (= 58.7, SD = 9.7). The skin* for the 23cultivars grown in Ohio, USA,,and in Italyranged from 61.9 to 67.6. The skin* value across eight cultivars grown in Missouri, USAwas much lower (= 39.4). This probably is because measurements were taken on whole fruits in Missouri that were stored frozen for 14 days before analysis, during which time skin was darker because browning likely occurred. Fruit skin color is a visual measure of ripeness in many fruits, but this does not appear to be the case in.

skin* values across the 27 cultivars ranged from −11.1 to 5.8 (= −5.8, SD = 4.8). Firmness

Texture is an important biophysical factor that affects consumer acceptance, marketability, shelf life, and is often the only used indicator of ripeness in fruits. In, softening is the only ripening index used to determineharvest, which is as true today as when it was first reported in 2003.Texture was reported for 29 named cultivars of(Table). Five manuscripts report texture that was generated by a force that penetrated through the skin,,,,and five report texture that was generated by a force that penetrated directly into the pulp after a small section of the skin was removed.,,,,This is an important distinction because comparisons of firmness through the skin and pulp are not comparable, as occurred in a recent article comparing wild harvestedto previous studies.

The first reported values for firmness on the pulp ofprobably was from fruit harvested in Maryland, USA during the 1991 growing season.This study suggested that underripefirmness was in the range of 10–30 N and ripefirmness was around 5 N. For comparison, a later study that performed firmness testing on the skinsuggested that the firmness of ripe fruit, which was described as fruit that exhibited some softness at harvest, was 8 N. The first study to report firmness on the skin of namedcultivarsreported values across two growing years (2004, 2005) reported a large variation in fruit firmness across the cultivars and suggested that fruit with firmness less than 15 N was suitable for immediate fresh market sales.

Using ‘below 15 N’ for market suitability or ‘below 8 N’ to indicate ripe fruit, eight of the 12 cultivars for which firmness on the skin was measured met these criteria: ‘PA‐Golden’, ‘Taylor’, ‘Wells’, and ‘Wilson’ exhibited mean firmness between 8 and 15 N, while ‘Ithica’, ‘Mary Foos Johnson’, ‘NC‐1’, ‘Sunflower’ exhibited mean firmness less than 8 N. What is unclear from the review of the manuscripts is whether the other four cultivars (‘KSU‐Atwood’, ‘Middletown’, ‘Shenandoah’, and ‘Taytwo’) are routinely harder when ripe. Using ‘around 5 N’ to indicate ripe fruit, 11 of the 21 cultivars for which firmness was measured by penetration directly into the pulp ranged from 4.1 N to 6.1 N, with the other ten ranging from 1.0 N to 3.8 N.

Cell wall composition

Cell wall glycan composition of two cultivars, ‘Greenriver Belle’ and ‘Susquehanna’, of freshpulp was reported in a single study.The study reported that the twocultivars are composed of a pectic fraction characterized by a homogalacturonan backbone and rhamnogalacturonans I and II, supported by hemicelluloses such as arabinogalactan I and II, glucomannan, and xylan. The study suggests that the composition of both of the cultivars is indicative of typical Type I primary cell walls of flowering dicots.

Although only two cultivars were studied, the cell walls in the two cultivars behaved differently during refrigerated storage. An increase in hemicellulosic arabinogalactans (I, II, III, IV) during refrigerated storage was observed in ‘Greenriver Belle’ but not ‘Susquehanna’. Arabinogalactans act as cellular adhesives to maintain plasma membrane‐cell wall integrity. Differences between cultivars with respect to cell wall degradation during storage may suggest cultivar‐specific softening during ripening and postharvest storage, perhaps due to a difference in cell wall‐modifying enzymes present in the primary cell walls. The activity of four cell wall modifying enzymes (endo‐(1,4)‐glucanase, endo‐β‐1,4‐mannase, pectin methylesterase, and polygalacturonase) from a composite of 20cultivars increased during ripening.

Enzymatic activity

The activity of seven of the enzymes in, alcohol dehydrogenase (ADH), alcohol acyl transferase (AAT), ascorbate peroxidase (APX), glutathione reductase (GR), lipoxygenase (LOX), peroxidase (POD), and phenylalanine ammonia lyase (PAL) was reported for a single cultivar (‘Taytwo’) in two studies.,Polyphenol oxidase (PPO) activity was reported for 16 cultivars across five studies.,,,,

Recent reviews describe the pathways of ethylene‐regulated LOX, AAT, and ADH that produce compounds related to ripeningand flavor and aroma deteriorationas the fruit is stored. Inof cultivar ‘Taytwo’, the relationship was less clear. Of the three enzymes, only LOX activity increased during ripening and there was no statistical relationship between ADH or LOX to AAT activity or production of volatile compounds.

APX, GR, and POD catalyze reactions to detoxify peroxides from plants.Galli. reports on the activity of these enzymes in cultivar ‘Taytwo’ but that the activity of these enzymes did not change during fruit ripening nor exhibit a trend during cold storage of wholefruit.These findings led the authors to conclude that the enzymes did not contribute to antioxidant protection during cold storage.

Shown in Table, PPO activity was reported for 16 namedcultivars.,,,,Research has characterized PPO activity incomposites and indicated that PPO is responsible for enzymatic browning during ripening, postharvest handling, storage, and processing.These authors determined that peak PPO activity was at pH 6.5–7.0, but activity remained at pH 5.5 and residual activity was observed at pH 4.0. Thus, PPO will be active inbecause the pH range ofwas 5.5–6.8 (= 6.1, SD = 0.3).

Brannanalso reported the enzyme kinetics for individual cultivars (Table) and used the data to suggest that low PPO activity and a low ratio of maximum velocity (Vmax) to Michaelis constant (Km) could indicate lower levels ofbrowning, suggesting that these reaction kinetics could be useful to understanding PPO behavior for individual cultivars. Applying these metrics, three cultivars, ‘SAA‐Zimmerman’, ‘IXL’, and ‘Lynn's Favorite’, would meet the requirement of low PPO activity and low Vmax/Km ratio. However, until critical values of PPO activity and Vmax to Km ratio can be established and connected to actual inhibition of postharvest browning, cultivar comparison remains inconclusive because all of the cultivars tested could be above or below the critical values.

Galli.also assayed for PAL activity with the notion that because PAL uses the amino acid‐phenylalanine to produce‐cinnamic acid, a required precursor in the biosynthesis of polyphenols in plants, increased PAL activity may provide more substrate for PPO. However, PAL did not increase during ripening or storage. Many potential polyphenolic substrates for PPO have been identified inpulp,,which are discussed in Section.

The activity of four cell wall modifying enzymes (endo‐(1,4)‐glucanase, endo‐β‐1,4‐mannase, pectin methylesterase, and polygalacturonase) from a composite of 20 cultivars increased during ripening.As noted previously in Section, there were differences between cultivars ‘Greenriver Belle’ and ‘Susquehanna’ with respect to cell wall degradation during refrigerated storagethat may suggest cultivar‐specific softening during ripening and postharvest storage. This could be due to a difference in these or other cell wall‐modifying enzymes. Thus, plant cellulases, specifically those that consume substrates previously identified by Brannan.in the cell walls ofsuch as xyloglucans, xylans, and glucomannan, could be a potential target for manipulation to improvepulp storage.

Volatile organic compounds

Early research on the aroma characteristics offruit from unknown cultivars in Japanand four experimental cultivarsindicated that the important class of aroma compounds inare methyl and ethyl esters, and important specific compounds are ethyl hexanoate, ethyl butanoate, and methyl octanoate. These molecules also are aroma contributors in a variety of tropical fruitsthat have aromas and flavors to whichis often compared. Aroma and headspace volatile compounds were identified for eight named cultivars of(‘KSU‐Atwood’, ‘Middletown’, ‘PA Golden’, ‘Taylor’, ‘Taytwo’, ‘Shenandoah’, ‘Wells’, and ‘Wilson’) and largely agree with the earlier findings.,These authors note that four compounds, methyl octanoate, ethyl octanoate, ethyl hexanoate and methyl hexanoate, predominate at harvest and during whole fruit storage after harvest.

The significant influence of esters on fruit aroma connects to the discussion of enzymatic activity (Section). AAT is one of the enzymes the catalyzes the formation and the hydrolysis of ester bonds in aroma compounds, and because aroma can be influenced by the availability of alcohols, aldehydes, lactones, and other minor compounds used in ester formation, the enzymes involved in the synthesis of these compounds, such as LOX and ADH, could play important roles.

CONCLUSIONS

As the industry grows, the need for meaningful indices offruit quality would be helpful. The bio‐properties onhave begun to establish some of the parameters for some of the cultivars, but significant gaps remain. Thus, bio‐properties that indicate specific quality prerequisites that will correlate to consumer acceptance of the fruit remains a long way off. An integration of two research thrusts would be useful. Research conducted that relies on the expertise of horticulture and plant scientists on namedcultivars could benefit from more detailed studies on how preharvest physiological and biochemical changes, mechanical injury, and disease affects peak ripeness levels and postharvest quality. These studies would be enhanced by collaboration into traditional food science research that considers the effect of processing, ingredient addition, packaging, and storage conditions. Establishing how preharvest environment, processing methods, and storage conditions offruit affect the nutritional content, shelf life, and texture, flavor, and overall quality could lead to the development of new‐based products and ingredients, expanding the value‐added market.
