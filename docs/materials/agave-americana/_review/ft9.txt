Introduction

The genusis endemic to the American continent. Some 75% of the knownspecies occur in Mexico (), where several of them are used to produce traditional distilled alcoholic beverages, such as… ().is the only non-distilled alcoholic beverage derived from the fermentation of agave sap, as recently reviewed by Escalante et al. is a colorless, sweet sap obtained from agave species such as., and() which, after spontaneous fermentation, yields the beverage known as. This beverage has interesting nutritional properties, as it contains essential amino acids, minerals such as K, Ca, Na, Fe, Cu, Mg, Se, and Zn; vitamin B complex and vitamin C, antioxidants (saponins and phenolic compounds), soluble fiber consisting of fructans from the plant and/or glucans produced by lactic acid bacteria, as well as probiotics (–). Fructans are fructose polymers that are indigestible by human gastrointestinal enzymes but that selectively stimulate beneficial gut microorganisms such asandamong others ().

Numerous scientific studies have demonstrated that the effect of fructan consumption on human health goes beyond their prebiotic activity, as they also contribute to reduce oxygen reactive species, promote calcium reabsorption, and are associated with a decrease in serum triglyceride and cholesterol concentrations (). Research on animal models has also demonstrated that a fructan-supplemented diet regulates the beneficial microbiota and decreases brain damage and deterioration (). All these properties may explain the health benefits ofconsumption in Mexican rural communities.

With regard to the agave industry, it is important to consider that, unlike, and other distilled beverages that require fructans hydrolysis by thermal treatment,fermentation is based on the fermentable sugars of, mostly sucrose, thus preserving agave fructans (). After centuries of traditional fermentation and decades of scientific research, the changes in concentration and composition (molecular weight distribution) of fructans inhave not been yet studied, particularly as regards the changes that take place during the agave productive lifetime. Sugar concentration in accumulatedis routinely reported; however, the changes incomposition during production after scraping and at different production stages have never been reported (,).

production starts when the agave plant reaches maturity (8 to 10 years) and immediately before flowering. At that time, the producer () performs a process known as “castration” or “layering,” consisting of removing the central leaves of the plant and carving a cavity (traditionally known as) to remove the apical meristem (or heart), which otherwise would form the inflorescence. Sap flows and accumulates in the(,), which is scraped twice a day after collecting theaccumulated, while the scraped material (is discarded. The castration process varies from region to region: in Huitzilac, a town in the State of Morelos, where this study was conducted, the central cavity is punctured without removing the central leaves, only extracting from the, the center of the base.

After castration, the agave is left to mature for up to 3 months or even longer, depending on the region and the manufacturing practice (). Producers in Huizilac leave the castrated agave to mature for 5 weeks before starting the scraping routine and the daily collection of. For this purpose, the sides of the stem are scraped with a metal spoon (specifically designed for the process, inducing sap to flow into theis collected once theis full, after 12–14 h of accumulation. This procedure is carried out twice a day over 4 to 9 months, depending on the plant size, until the plant dies.

The accumulatedis traditionally extracted from theusing a sort of large straw made from a dried pumpkin (, but modern production processes use pumps. Afterwards,is transported to the fermentation room (), where fermentation takes place after inoculation with freshly produced. The whole process has been recently reviewed by Escalante et al. ().

The sugars profile offrom severalspecies has been the subject of various studies (,,,). However, no detailed information is yet available on the relationship betweencomposition and the plant productive stage, nor on changes incomposition during post-scraping accumulation. Ortiz-Basurto et al. () described changes incomposition during the agave productive lifetime, concluding that its components profile varies little over time. However, as described in this manuscript, their sampling procedures cannot be compared.

We hereby describe the evolution ofcomposition in three-producing agave plants, in terms of the concentration of glucose, fructose, sucrose, and the concentration and molecular weight distribution of fructans. We also describe the changes occurring duringaccumulation in theand through the entire productive lifetime. We carried out an overall quantification of the process in eachplant to identify the fate of fructans, in eitheror, and to quantify the total amount of sucrose produced. A fourth plant was analyzed as a reference to describe the initial concentration and molecular weight profile of fructans in a pine prior to production. We also analyzed the influence of the microbiota in. This is the first time that this ancestral process is thoroughly examined and described, providing important conclusions that may affect the way the extraction procedure evolves in order to improve the nutritional quality of the product and the control of the fermentation process.

The fact that fructans are found not only in the vacuole, but also in the apoplast (), supports our explanation above. We can also speculate that fructans are mainly drained from inter-cellular spaces rather than from vacuoles, due to the difficulty of removing them from this organelle, where they are protected by the cell wall, the plasmatic membrane, and the tonoplast. This finding could have important consequences given the nutritional role of fructans, and the fate of the fructans remaining in the plant. Identification of Oligosaccharides in Accumulated

We carried out two different analyses based on the specificity of hydrolytic enzymes to determine whether the OS found in type-1 and type-2are either fructose or glucose polymers. For the first analysis, samples of accumulatedwere subjected to dextranase treatment, as several lactic acid bacteria (e.g.,, etc.) capable of synthesizing dextrans or isomalto-oligosaccharides from sucrose through glucosyltransferases have been described as agave endophytes (,). Based on a similar hypothesis, for the second analysis thesamples were treated with Fructozyme, a combination of endo- and exo-fructanases able to hydrolyze fructans such as inulin or levan. The accumulation of either glucose or fructose, respectively, following these enzymatic reactions, along with the reduction of their HPLC signals, would indicate the synthesis of gluco- or fructo-oligosaccharides duringaccumulation.

The dextranase treatment did not change the oligosaccharide profile (). In contrast, almost all the complex OS were hydrolyzed after the Fructozyme treatment (). These results indicate that OS present in accumulated type-1 and type-2are fructo-oligosaccharides with either β2-1 or β2-6 structure, but not an isomalto-oligosaccharide, α1-6-type structure, as suggested by the common presence of dextran in fermented.

These results demonstrate the important activity of lactic acid bacteria that initiate the fermentation ofin the plant, synthesizing complex sugars (mainly fructo-oligosaccharides) from sucrose, as observed in type-1 and type-2. These sugars are produced from glucose released from sucrose, which also accounts for the contents of reducing sugars in accumulated.

Changes in Sugar Concentration inand Scraped Tissue During the Productive Lifetime of Agave Plants

Sucrose and Reducing Sugars

Although the concentration of sugars in both freshand scraped tissue would be expected to change over the plant production lifetime, neither sugar composition nor its changes have been reported so far. The ANOVAs showed that sugar concentration incollected after scraping and in the scraped tissue varies significantly over the several months of the plant productive lifetime.

Sucrose concentration incollected after scraping increased with time from 54, 99.2 to 123 g Lin the earliest collected samples of the three plants, to reach a peak level after several weeks of production (147, 125 and 134 g L, respectively). Although the peak sucrose concentration varied across plants, in all cases it was reached in the first two months of production and decreased thereafter until the end of the agave productive lifetime ().

We believe that this behavior inis a consequence of the stress caused to the plant by the “castration” procedure followed by the physical damage inflicted with daily scraping. In response, the plant carries out photosynthesis intensively to supply sucrose to the nutrient-deprived, damaged organs. The increasing sucrose content inmay also be a consequence of carbohydrate mobilization.

When, after several months, the agave plant reaches the latest production stage, the leaves nearest theare removed to provide easier access to. The removal of the remaining photosynthetic organs causes sucrose content into decrease. In the final stage ofproduction, the agave leaves, weakened by the systematic physical damage and the draining of their energy source, reach senescence (see). These data confirm the centuries-old traditional knowledge:are a source of sucrose,literally means “honey-water.”

Unlike, extracts of grinded scraped tissue contain glucose and fructose, besides sucrose. The proportion of reducing sugars increases as production time proceeds, undoubtedly as a result from invertase and/or Fructan exohydrolase (FEH) activity. Large amounts of simple sugars were observed inbetween weeks 5 and 7.

The largest Mn and Mw values were observed at week 15 in P1, week 17 in P3, and at the last production week in P2. Thus, the time needed to reach the highest polymer molar mass in number and weight varies between plants. By comparing DPn and DPw, we concluded that fructan composition ofis heterogeneous (PI > 1), with the polydispersity index varying over the production season. Fructanare less disperse at the beginning of the agave productive lifetime and become poly-disperse as the plant is exploited. This transition takes place between the first third and the midpoint of the productive lifetime, but varies between plants.

The highest degree of polymerization by mass (DPw) of fructan in freshwas recorded after 15 weeks in P1 (2.2), 27 weeks in P2 (2.6), and 17 weeks in P3 (2.6) with a DPw of 6.1, 5.1, and 4.6, respectively. Based on the degree of polymerization, we can conclude that FOS are the predominant fructans in; this is consistent with what Ortiz-Basurto et al. () reported. The changes in the degree of polymerization of fructan during the production lifetime seem to be associated to plant stress, as discussed below.

Scraped Tissue

Little attention has been paid so far to the scraped tissue,, an abundant residue ofproduction. Escobedo-García et al. () recently proposed its use as a functional ingredient in supplemented cookies. However, as described here, the production stage at which bagasse is collected should be carefully considered for any application.shows that a normal fructan Mw distribution is never attained in the scraped tissue, as observed in the reference plant stem (P4). Instead, the bimodal distribution denotes structural changes associated with production stress. Interestingly, except for the intermediate collections, fructans indid attain a normal Mw distribution.

Fructans found in scraped tissue have a higher molecular weight than those in. The largest fructan molecules found in scraped tissue had DPn = 9 and DPw = 11, whereas the largest polymers found inhad DPn = 3 and DPw=6. These differences in the degree of polymerization and molecular weight betweenand scraped tissue further confirm our previous hypothesis that fructans income primarily from the apoplast and not from vacuoles, where larger fructans are stored.

Sugar Concentration and Degree of Polymerization in the Agave

Establishing a direct association between fructans inand those in the plant is challenging. However, we examined fructan composition and distribution in a mature plant as a baseline for comparison vs. those in, and in the pine. The core of the pine corresponds to the base of theand is cut off during agave “castration” to halt flowering and ensure that the carbohydrate reservoir of the plant is conserved forproduction (). The vegetative meristem and its surrounding tissue (young developing leaves) are also removed, leaving a cavity that serves as a container foraccumulation.

shows the concentration of simple sugars and fructan in samples from different sections of the reference mature agave pine before castration. Simple sugars are present at thebase, accounting for 20–50% of the total carbohydrate concentration in this section, while fructans account for the rest. Sugar contents varied between the different parts of thebase. Concentration of simple sugars was lower in the core and increased toward the developing leaves, while fructan concentration showed the opposite pattern. As regards the radial distribution (see the cross section in), the concentration of simple sugars was lower in the sections closer to the agave stem. The amount of fructans in the first two sections was similar but increased in the region closer to the agave stem.

No fructose or glucose was detected in the sections close to the base of the stem. Fructans constituted 307.8, 397.9, 531.6, and 565.9 mg/g of dry mass in the center of the sections S3.2, S4, S5, and S6, respectively (), while fructose, glucose, and sucrose accounted for 39.9, and 49.8 mg/g of dry pine mass in the first two sections (S3.2, S4) of the stem. Finally, sucrose concentration in sections S5 and S6 was 28.5 and 18.5 mg/g dry mass.

These results are similar to those reported by García-Curbelo et al. () and were to be expected if we consider that the agave stem is a reservoir organ. The concentrations of fructose, glucose, and sucrose were lower in areas closer to the base of the stem, while fructan content increased. García-Curbelo et al. () also showed that the concentration and degree of fructan polymerization was higher in the region closer to the base of the stem, while simple sugars concentrate in the upper sections, away from the base. As described above, the fructan Mw distribution varied between pine sections.illustrates that in terms of DPn and DPw, that polymers are small in the first stem section and larger in the lower sections (DPn:13.4 and DPw:19.8), where a clear difference in degree of polymerization can be observed (see also).

As explained in the previous section, the size and irregular (mainly bimodal) Mw distribution of fructans in scraped tissue depart from the normal distribution observed in the reference stem (). These results demonstrate that these modifications involve fructan hydrolysis, as the degree of polymerization (in number and mass) in scraped tissue from an-producing pine is considerably lower than in the intact stem.

As discussed previously, fructan size and concentration increase toward the base of the stem in the intact reference pine, while the lowest concentrations in the scraped tissue were recorded at the end of the plant productive lifetime, when the stem base is reached, suggesting fructan hydrolysis by FEH enzymes in the plant. Such hydrolysis is expected, given the huge stress to which the plant is subjected over the entire period ofproduction, particularly at the base of the stem where fructan concentration is higher. The fructose or FOS resulting from hydrolysis may then be transported to the apoplast throughcell walls to protect the plant from the damage produced by scraping and exposure to the environment. Based on the studies by Livingston and Henson (), the hydrolysis and mobilization of fructan likely start with castration in order to fulfill an energetic role, avoid dryness, or induce an immune response (), thus affecting the fructan profile and concentration inand.

Total Sugar Yield During the Production Process

The total amount of sucrose produced over the entire agave production lifetime was estimated based on the assumption that sucrose concentration inwas the same in the two daily collections (24 h). This assumption is based on our results (see section Daily Changes in Aguamiel Composition above) showing that sucrose concentration remained almost constant from 7:00 to 14:00 h. This value is likely an overestimation, since plants are drained twice a day and CAM plants capture more COduring nighttime and increase gluconeogenesis during daytime (). Similarly, the total amount of fructans produced during the entire agave production lifetime was estimated based on the assumption that the fructan concentration measured in the first 15 mL ofcollected after scraping is the same for the first 200 mL. This assumption is based on our results (see section Fructan Dilution in) showing that fructans occur in the highest concentration in the initial volume and are still present, but in small amounts, after 200 mL have been collected. Thus, this is also an approximate figure.

This corresponds to an overall average of 9–11% w/v of sucrose, and 0.2–0.6% w/v of fructans in thecollected. Concentration of simple sugars in scraped tissue are higher than in the intact plant, strengthening the hypothesis that the plants respond to stress by hydrolyzing fructans, which are then recovered in.

The estimated total amount of sugars (fructose, glucose, sucrose, and fructans) in scraped tissue ranged between 4 and 6% w/w of total wet mass; total carbohydrates in the intact pine accounted for 17% w/w. According to our estimates, the amount of fructans lost in the scraped tissue represents 1.3, 0.5, and 0.9 kg, or 30%, 22.7%, and 30% of the total amount originally in the pine. The amount of fructans lost in scraped tissue represents a significant waste in the standardproduction process, which has been rarely considered so far.

Concluding Remarks

We examined the composition of soluble carbohydrates, especially fructans, during the entire process ofharvesting. We believe that this new information would warrant a detailed revision of the procedures used for producingand its final product () in order to increase the availability of fructans, one of the richest nutriments in agave. We showed thatscraping is the main source of the fructans found in. Our results could inform the design and adoption of improved harvesting practices that yield higher concentrations of fructans inas well as innovative practices for recovering the huge amount of fructans lost in the scraped tissue (). We also found that the taxonomic profile of the fresh aguamiel compared to themicrobiota differed mainly in the abundance of thegenus which species might play an important role in defining the different carbohydrate profiles in aguamiel.
