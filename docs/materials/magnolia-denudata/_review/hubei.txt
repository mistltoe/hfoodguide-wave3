Introduction

,, andare deciduous trees of the genus Magnolia in the family Magnoliaceae, primarily cultivated in the provinces of Anhui, Henan, Sichuan, and Hubei. In China, their dried flower buds are used medicinally as “Xinyi”, which is an important traditional Chinese medicinal material widely used in pharmaceutical preparations and chemical industries, such as Xinyi rhinitis pill, Shisanwei Xinyi nasal drops, Xinyi essential oil and Xinyi aromatherapy, etc. Modern medicine shows that Xinyi has excellent efficacy in treating nasal diseases. Therefore, “Xinyi” has become an important representative natural plant medicine for treating rhinitis and has important economic value in China.

As the primary active ingredients in many herbal medicines, the volatile oils offer a range of health benefits, including antibacterial properties, anti-inflammatory effects, cellular protection against damage, and anticancer activity. These oils are the key quality markers of “Xinyi”, with the terpenoids such as 1,8-Cineole, camphor, geranylene, and pinene comprising the majority. This finding shows that among the herb’s primary pharmacological components, there are compounds that effectively prevent rhinitis. Notably, 1,8-Cineole is particularly significant, known for its stimulating, cooling, and anti-inflammatory effects, and its high concentration is closely linked to the efficacy of “Xinyi”, making it a crucial indicator of its volatile oil quality. The accumulation of volatile oils is influenced by various factors, with internal factors, particularly plant growth and development, playing a dominant role. In the Magnoliaceae family, the oil cells serve as a type of secretory cell and the primary sites for the synthesis and accumulation of volatile oils. The quantity and quality of volatile oils are directly correlated with the growth of these cells. However, the studies on the oil cells of “Xinyi” remain limited, with existing research primarily focused on.

The complex and lengthy history of “Xinyi” medicinal herbs, coupled with the occasionally ambiguous origins of their raw plant species, significantly limits their production and clinical applications. The varieties of “Xinyi” from the same geographical region demonstrate the considerable variation in the volatile oil content and chemical composition, resulting in the significant differences in herb quality. Although the provenance of “Xinyi” herbs has been documented in the Pharmacopoeia multiple occasions, a systematic framework for assessing their potency remains undeveloped. Recent research on the volatile oil in theflower buds has focused primarily on its origin, species, extraction methods, and pharmacological activity, whereas few studies onandare medicinally valued for their flower buds, withparticularly under-researched.

has become the predominant species of “Xinyi” medicinal herbs, accounting for approximately 80% of sales, according to the survey by Wang et al.. To further investigate the quality differences in volatile oil among various species and growth stages, we examined,, andsourced from Hubei Province. The volatile oil was extracted from flower buds at different growth stages using the steam distillation. The micro-technology and GC-MS was utilized to observe and quantify the content and primary components of the volatile oil and analyze the changes in their composition. This study aimed to elucidate the relationship between the variations in the volatile oil content, its main components, and the development of oil cells across different species and growth stages. Ultimately, this research was conducted to identify the optimal species and harvesting time for flower buds, providing a theoretical foundation for the scientific advancement of “Xinyi”.

Materials and methods

Sample collection and pre-processing

This article utilizes flower buds of “Xinyi” at various stages of development, collected from Xianning, Wuhan, and Enshi in Hubei between May 2022 and March 2023. The plants were identified as,, andby Engineer Wenguang Wang from the Xishuangbanna Tropical Botanical Garden of the Chinese Academy of Sciences and Lecturer Mingli Hu from Hubei University of Science and Technolog. The specimens of,, andwere placed in the herbarium of College of Pharmacy in Hubei University of Science and Technology, named B20220515, B20220516 and B20220517, respectively. Following collection, the samples were cleaned with ultrapure water, dried in an oven at 37 °C, crushed, and then sieved through a 60-mesh sieve. Subsequently, they were stored in a sealed bag at room temperature for future use. Detailed collection information for the samples is provided in Table. The samples of,, andwere designated as a, b, and c, respectively, while the various developmental phases were numbered from 1 to 6.

Evaluation of representative indicators

External morphological observation

The buds in different stages of this study were divided according to the growth months, and sampling was carried out in the 1st, 3rd, 5th, 7th, 9th, and 10th months of bud growth, respectively, dividing them into 6 stages. Twenty dried flower buds in good condition were randomly selected from each of the six distinct developmental phases. The lengths of each flower bud were measured, and these processes were repeated three times to obtain the mean values (in centimeters).

Measurement of flower bud dry weight

Twenty dried flower buds from each specimen were randomly selected at six distinct developmental phases while in a healthy state. The dry weights of each flower bud were then measured, and this process was repeated three times for each specimen to obtain the mean values (g).

Oil cell observation

Observation of oil cell density

We selected 1 cm × 1 cm samples from six different growth stages of healthytepals across three species. These samples were treated in an aqueous solution containing 1–8% sodium hydroxide for a duration of five to ten days at room temperature. After achieving tissue transparency, the samples were carefully placed on slides and covered with coverslips to prevent the formation of air bubbles. Initially, the tissue was rinsed with distilled water. The samples were observed with a Leica DMLB microscope. The method refers to Hu et al..

Histochemical localization of secondary metabolites

We selected 1 cm × 1 cm samples from six different growth stages of healthy magnolia tepals across three species. Place the above sample on a glass slide and gently crush it with tweezers until the oil cells are free. The histolocalization of lipid was determined by staining the Sudan III (0.5% Sudan III powder is dissolved in 70% ethanol) for 5 min and then rinsed with distilled water for 1 min; The histolocalization of terpenoids was determined by staining the cryomicrotome sections with NaDi (10% α-naphthol in 40% ethanol and 1% dimethyl para-phenylenediamine chloride in 0.05 M phosphate buffer) for 30 min and then rinsed with distilled water for 1 min. The samples were subsequently stripped from the lower epidermis and observed with a Leica DMLB microscope.

Paraffin sections

Conventional procedures were employed to create paraffin slices. We selected 1 cm × 1 cm samples from six different growth stages of healthy magnolia tepals across three species. Then, samples were immersed in a 70% FAA fixing solution (composed of 90 ml of ethanol, 5 ml of glacial acetic acid, and 5 ml of 37% formaldehyde) for more than a day. Subsequently, the tenth slices were extracted and gradually dehydrated using 80%, 90%, 95%, and 100% alcohol for one to two hours, followed by permeation and embedding in paraffin, resulting in a section thickness of 5 to 10 μm. The slices were then dried at 40 °C in an oven, dyed with saffron-solid green, and sealed with neutral gum. Sections were examined and photographed using a Leica DMLB microscope, and the dimensions of the oil cells were measured. The method refers to Liang et al..

Volatile oil extraction

The extraction of volatile oils was conducted in accordance with the most recent Chinese Pharmacopoeia (Part IV). Initially, 400 ml of distilled water was added to 1000 ml round-bottomed flasks containing 10 g of powder from each of the six samples at various growth stages. A volatile oil tester was connected to a reflux condenser tube, and the mixtures were heated for five hours using an electric jacket. Following this, the extracted substances were transferred into a round-bottomed flask. The distillate was then allowed to stand for separation, after which it was extracted with methylene chloride, dried with anhydrous sodium sulfate, and subsequently heated in a water bath to remove the methylene chloride. The resulting yellow volatile oil was collected, weighed, and stored in a refrigerator at -20 °C for future use.

GC-MS analysis

Following the extraction of each sample, the volatile oil was combined with dichloromethane to prepare a test solution with a concentration of 800 µg/mL and an injection volume of 1.0 µL. The analytical conditions were as follows: an Agilent 7890 A GC/5975 C MS instrument was employed for GC-MS analysis, utilizing an HP-5 MS capillary column (0.25 mm × 30 m, 0.1 μm) as the chromatographic column. The initial column temperature was set at 60 °C and held for two minutes, after which it was increased to 115 °C at a rate of 3 °C per minute. The temperatures of the quadrupole and injection port were maintained at 150 °C and 200 °C, respectively. Subsequently, the temperature was raised to 130 °C at a rate of 6 °C per minute, followed by an increase to 200 °C at a rate of 3 °C per minute, and finally to 250 °C at a rate of 10 °C per minute, which was maintained for an additional two minutes. High-purity gas was used as the carrier. Electron impact (EI) was employed as the ion source, with a temperature of 230 °C and a scanning mass range of 50 to 550 AMU. The flow rate was set to 1.0 mL/min with a shunt ratio of 10:1.

Statistical data analysis

Statistical analysis of oil cell density

The density of the oil cells was examined and digitally recorded. The density (n/m) and the diameter size (µm) of the oil cells were counted, measured and averaged from 15 samples. An analysis of variation (ANOVA) and plotting was performed with Origin software. The results were considered statistically significant for<0.05.

Statistical analysis of volatile oil content

The yield of the volatile oils was determined in triplicate, and the results were expressed as mean values. The percentage of the volatile oil yield was calculated as the weight of volatile oils divided by the weight of flower buds. An analysis of variation (ANOVA) and plotting was performed with Origin software. The results were considered statistically significant for<0.05.

Statistical analysis of GC-MS

The peaks in the effluent were analyzed to identify the chemicals present, utilizing the NIST 14 mass spectrum library search alongside relevant literature. The relative concentration of each component was subsequently determined through GC-MS peak area normalization. The method refers to Hu et al.. An analysis of variation (ANOVA) and plotting was performed with Origin software. The results were considered statistically significant for<0.05.

HCA analysis

Hierarchical clustering is a cluster analysis method that seeks to construct a cluster hierarchy. Hierarchical cluster analysis and the Euclidean distance were applied in this study. To establish the distance matrix for the chromatographic area and the samples for the cluster analysis from the GC–MS data, cluster analysis and dendrogram creation were performed with SPSS version 18.0 software (SPSS Inc., Chicago, USA).

PCA analysis

The PCA is performed by singular-value decomposition of the fingerprint data array. Reducing the number of variables is one of PCA’s objectives to make it possible in visualizing the data in the multivariate data set. Similarly, common peaks area in HPLC were selected as the measurement values for principal component analysis. The SPSS was used to perform the principal component analysis and to construct the scattered scores plot of the results.

Comparison of oil cell density and distribution in the three species of

The primary components of “Xinyi” flowers include the gynoecium, androecium, receptacles, and tepals. Compared with other tissues, the oil cells in these flowers are uniformly distributed, predominantly concentrated in the perianth segments, and form a cohesive, nearly circular structure. Using the paraffin section method, we conducted a comparative analysis of tepals from the threespecies. The extraction of oil with organic solvents contributed to the vacuolation of oil cells identified during the paraffin sectioning process. In the tepals ofandat the second stage, the oil cells were significantly larger than those in(Fig.(a-1)-(a-3)). The number of oil cells and amount of volatile oil in flower buds of different “Xinyi” types at various growth stages were compared by counting and observing the density of oil cells in the tepals, constituting the majority of flower buds. Comparison of the three species ofbud volatile oil types and the dynamics of their accumulation

Figure(b-1)-(b-9) illustrates the different morphological features of the oil cells observed within the same field of view. We adopted the tissue transparency method to examine the volatile oils present in the tepals of flower buds from three distinct types of 3 Magnolia plants at various growth stages. This approach enhanced our understanding of the temporal accumulation of volatile oils in the oil cells. According to Hu et al.1, five phases were identified in the growth and development of oil cells: the volatile oil-free phase, the volatile oil production phase, the volatile oil accumulation phase, the volatile oil saturation phase, and the volatile oil disintegration phase. The oil cells in the perianth segments of the three Magnolia buds had not yet formed during the volatile oil-free period (Fig.a-1, b-1, and c-1). During this period, the small oil droplets gradually accumulated in the oil cells, forming the small light-yellow droplets (Fig.a-2, b-2, and c-2). These tiny droplets continued to merge during the volatile oil accumulation phase, creating the large, nearly spherical droplets (Fig.a-3, b-3, and c-3). Following the round and full state of the oil cells during the volatile oil saturation phase (Fig.a-4, b-4, and c-4), they entered the volatile oil disintegration phase, during which the volatile oil gradually deteriorated, forming a blackish-brown hue (Fig.a-5, b-5, and c-5). In addition, we utilized Sudan III and NaDi reagents to perform histochemical localization of oil cells. The results indicated that Sudan III effectively stained the volatile oil in oil cells orange-yellow (Fig.a-6, b-6, and c-6), suggesting that the volatile oil is abundant in lipid substances. Conversely, the NaDi reagent stained the volatile oil in oil cells blue-purple (Fig.a-7, b-7, and c-7), indicating a high content of terpene substances in the volatile oil

The oil cells with varying degrees of accumulation in the perianth segments at different growth stages across three species of “Xinyi” were counted to thoroughly investigate the relationship between the oil cell density and the volatile oil accumulation in these segments. The results, presented in Table, presented that the highest oil cell densities for,, andwere observed during the fourth, fifth, and third growth stages, respectively, at which point the highest proportion of oil cells contributing to the volatile oil saturation was also indicated (Fig.c). As the plants grew and the flower buds developed until blooming, the area of mature tepals demonstrated the rapid increase. Consequently, the sharp decrease in the density of the oil cells was observed, with the largest proportion of oil cells observed during the volatile oil disintegration period.

Discussion

Effects of different varieties and different development stages on the content and composition of Xinyi volatile oil

The content of active ingredients in medicinal plants is significantly influenced by factors such as variety and developmental stage. Consequently, the levels of secondary metabolites in medicinal materials vary at different harvest times. Oil cells, as part of the secretory tissue, serve as the primary site for the synthesis and accumulation of volatile oils in “Xinyi”. Numerous studies have indicated that the density of oil cells and the degree of volatile oil accumulation within these cells fluctuate with changes in growth stage, season, and environmental conditions, representing the main intrinsic factors affecting volatile oil content. Significant differences in oil cell density and volatile oil accumulation have been observed at various growth stages of the same plant. Collectively, these studies demonstrate that the yield and quality of volatile oil are closely linked to oil cell density, exhibiting a positive correlation with both the density and accumulation of volatile oil. In cinnamon, mature leaves exhibit the highest oil cell density, the greatest proportion of oil cells during the volatile oil saturation stage, and the highest production of volatile oil. In contrast, young leaves have a lower density of oil cells and reduced volatile oil accumulation, while the volatile oil content in older leaves gradually degrades, leading to a decrease in yield. Our results further illustrate that volatile oil production is closely associated with the density and accumulation of oil cells in petals across different growth stages. The volatile oil content typically peaks when oil cell density reaches its maximum, coinciding with the highest proportion of oil cells in the volatile oil saturation phase.

The composition and relative contents of various secondary metabolites in medicinal plants are influenced by different developmental stages and varieties. During growth and development, plants utilize photosynthetic products to synthesize a range of secondary metabolites, with distinct compounds typically being produced at different developmental stages. In certain plant species, a notable accumulation of volatile compounds occurs prior to the full expansion of the organs. Once the organs reach full maturity, the accumulation of essential oils ceases, and subsequently, these oils may be lost due to evaporation, degradation, or a reduction in content. Research indicates that the number of volatile compounds in lavender flowers is greater in summer compared to autumn. The volatile oil content ofincreases systematically across five consecutive growth stages, peaking just prior to the bloom period. This research demonstrates that the components of volatile oils continue to evolve with the growth and development of the plants. The findings indicate a significant correlation between the developmental stage of three magnolia buds and their chemical composition. Notably, 1,8-cineole, the key component of Xinyi, exhibits a distinct trend of initially increasing and then decreasing (Fig.b). Inand, the content of 1,8-cineole is elevated during winter but experiences a sharp decline in late winter and early spring, coinciding with the onset of flowering. This compound possesses a unique aroma and may be released in substantial quantities when the flowers bloom to attract pollinators, leading to a sudden reduction in its concentration. Furthermore, the 1,8-cineole content inandis significantly higher than that in, indicating considerable variations in the quality of Xinyi from different sources. In summary, both the variety and developmental stage of the plants significantly influence the volatile oil content and composition of Xinyi.

Quality evaluation of Xinyi produced in Hubei

To obtain high-yield and high-quality traditional Chinese medicine, it is generally recommended to harvest when the content of active ingredients is at its peak. Typically, floral medicinal materials are harvested before flowering, as the active ingredient content reaches its highest levels at this stage.has been identified as the primary species of “Xinyi”, with the volatile oil in the flower buds being its main component. According to the Chinese Pharmacopoeia Committee, the volatile oil is a key quality indicator for “Xinyi”, and harvesting should occur in late winter and early spring, specifically before the flowers open. The study found that during the initial growth stage, the volatile oils in the three magnolia species were primarily in the non-volatile oil and volatile oil formation stages. At this stage, the secondary metabolism of aromatic substances is predominantly regulated by primary metabolism, leading to a relatively low accumulation of secondary metabolites of volatile oils. As the flower buds continue to grow and develop, the formation of secondary metabolites is supported by an adequate supply of primary metabolites, resulting in a peak in volatile oil content at a specific time. In the final developmental stage, a significant decrease in oil cell density was observed, and the opening of flower buds led to the volatilization of aromatic compounds, thereby causing a notable reduction in volatile oil content. Consequently, it was demonstrated that the volatile oil concentrations of the three magnolia species exhibited a pattern of initially increasing and then decreasing across different developmental stages. Specifically,,, andreached their highest volatile oil content during the fourth, fifth, and third growth stages, respectively.

The content of active ingredients in medicinal plants is influenced by various developmental stages and seasonal changes. It is widely accepted that the stage with the highest concentration of active ingredients represents the optimal harvest period. For instance, the concentrations of flavonoids and terpene lactones in Ginkgo biloba were analyzed across different months and origins, with October identified as the most favorable harvest time. Similarly, the optimal harvest period for honeysuckle buds aligns with the large white and white stages of flowering, during which the concentrations of lignans and chlorogenic acids peak. Furthermore, Hu et al.indicated that the volatile oil content and overall active ingredient levels inandflower buds were highest in February and October, respectively. Extreme seasonal temperatures, whether excessively high or low, are believed to negatively impact the accumulation of volatile oils. In this study, volatile oils in the flower buds of three different “Xinyi” varieties produced in Hubei were analyzed using GC-MS, resulting in the identification of a total of 38, 44, and 59 components. The results indicated that the volatile oil content ofspecies, specifically,, and, exhibited a trend of initial increase followed by a decrease in 1,8-cineole content.demonstrated the highest volatile oil content and 1,8-cineole levels during the fourth stage (a4), whilepeaked in both volatile oil and 1,8-cineole content during the fifth stage (b5).achieved its highest volatile oil and 1,8-cineole content in the third stage (c3); however, its overall volatile oil content was notably low and did not meet the Chinese Pharmacopoeia standard of 1%. In summary, among the Xinyi sources from Hubei,andare recommended for medicinal use, with optimal harvest periods identified as the fourth stage (a4) and fifth stage (b5), respectively.
