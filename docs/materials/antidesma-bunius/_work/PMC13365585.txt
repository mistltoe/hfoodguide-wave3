Introduction

Urban green spaces, vital for biodiversity and ecosystem services, face habitat loss driven by global climate change and intense human activities. These vegetated spaces, defined by their vegetation, provide essential resources for urban fauna. The genusserves as a key food source for urban birds and forest animal species, as. Its two species,and, are globally widespread.occurs in Guangdong, Fujian, Sichuan, Yunnan, Guizhou, and Guangxi provinces (China), as well as in Japan, the Indochina Peninsula, and Australia.., endemic to China, ranges from the Qinling Mountains and areas south of the Huaihe River basin to northern Guangdong and Guangxi, commonly inhabiting the Yangtze River plain. Both species are heliophilic yet moderately shade-tolerant, preferring warm climates;.exhibits greater cold hardiness. They tolerate a range of soil conditions but thrive in moist, fertile substrates, and are resilient to drought, poor soils, and waterlogging. Characterized by robust root systems, high wind resistance, dense foliage, and attractive form, both species are valued as shade, embankment, and street trees, making them ideal for natural and urban “green barrier” landscapes. Furthermore, the fruits ofare often used for making wines, and the seeds yield 30%−54% oil. However, morphological and ecological similarities between.and.often lead to misidentification. In this study, we differentiate these species using leaf, bark, branch, flower, fruit, and deciduous characteristics, along with complete chloroplast genome sequences.

Advances in cost-effective chloroplast sequencing and bioinformatics now enable extensive research into plant chloroplast genomes for species identification, phylogenetic reconstruction, and evolutionary studies. Chloroplasts, the photosynthetic organelles of plants, possess a semi-autonomous genetic system comprising a circular double-stranded DNA molecule. Compared to mitochondrial and nuclear genomes, chloroplast genomes exhibit high sequence and structural conservation. Typically, they display a quadripartite structure: a large single-copy (LSC) region, a small single-copy (SSC) region, and two inverted repeats (IRa and IRb), as seen in groups like algae and families including Sterculiaceae, Sterculiaceae, and Euphorbiaceae. While conserved, chloroplast genomes exhibit sufficient interspecific variation for robust phylogenetic and population genetic analyses. Over the past three decades, numerous phylogenetic studies using chloroplast DNA sequence data have contributed to our understanding of the evolutionary relationships within the genus of(Phyllanthaceae).

Molecular phylogenetic analyses have profoundly influenced and clarified contemporary taxonomic frameworks. According to the APG II system (2003), Euphorbiaceae are now delineated to encompass solely uniovulate subfamilies, while Phyllanthoideae have been reclassified as the family Phyllanthaceae, and Oldfieldioideae as Picrodendraceae. A fundamental systematic split within Euphorbiaceae sensu- lato centers on ovule number, distinguishing a cluster of two biovulate subfamilies (Phyllanthoideae and Oldfieldioideae) from three uniovulate subfamilies (Acalyphoideae, Crotonoideae, and Euphorbioideae). Morphologically based phylogenetic inquiries into Phyllanthoideae have incorporated examinations of thealliance, with limited taxonomic sampling of Phyllanthaceae employed as outgroups in studies of Oldfieldioideae (Picrodendraceae) and Celastraceae. Investigations into wood anatomy, pollen morphology, seed coat ultrastructure, and leaf venation have revealed several traits of putative systematic importance, although these have not yet been incorporated within a phylogenetic context. Recent advances in the molecular systematics of Phyllanthaceae have marked significant progress. Wurdack et al. (2004, 2005) conducted phylogenetic reconstructions using plastidand-DNA sequences, generating trees that include 54 of the 59 recognized genera and exhibit strong resolution and support across most nodes. Kathriarachchi et al. (2005) expanded on these efforts via a multi-gene analysis, employing five markers (plastid,,′,, and nuclear) for a comparable genus-level sampling. Their work augmented earlier studies based solely on(Wurdack et al., 2004) or more limited taxon sets forand(Samuel et al., 2005). These analyses demonstrated concordance between plastid and nuclear gene phylogenies, and their integration produced a highly robust and well-supported phylogenetic framework. Recently, Bouman et al. (2021) proposed a revision of the polyphyletic genusby dividing it into several monophyletic genera, based on a phylogenetic analysis using nuclear (,) and chloroplast (,-,-) markers. The systematic location of Phyllanthaceae has been widely recognized, but many problems remain in the delimitation and classification of its genera. With ongoing advancements in bioinformatics, future studies leveraging complete chloroplast genome sequences are essential to further refine the phylogenetic and systematic understanding of Phyllanthaceae. Nevertheless, chloroplast genome data in Phyllanthaceae are still limited.

This study presents the first parallel comparative analysis of the complete chloroplast genome sequences ofand its sister species,, integrating anatomical data to assess genomic and morphological divergence. Using these data, we construct an expanded phylogeny within the Euphorbiaceae and Phyllanthaceae and elucidate the relationship betweenand. This integrative comparative analysis provides a foundation for understanding chloroplast genome evolution and phylogenetic relationships within the Phyllanthaceae.

SSR and repeat sequence analysis

In this study, a total of 291 simple sequence repeats (SSRs) were identified in the chloroplast genome of, with repeat lengths ranging from 8 to 20 bp (Supplementary Table S1). These included 189 mononucleotide, 15 dinucleotide, 82 trinucleotide, and 5 tetranucleotide repeats (Fig.a and Supplementary Table S2), and 47 SSRs were longer than 10 bp. The longest SSR was a single-base T repeat, with a length of 20 bp. There were 146 SSRs based on A/T, accounting for 53.09% of all SSRs (Fig.a). The SSRs were predominantly located in the LSC region (including,,,,,,,, and), the inverted repeat (IR) regions (including,,,,,, and), and the SSC region (including,,,,,,,,,,,, and). Of the 291 SSRs identified, 193 (66.3%) were located in the LSC region, 50 (17.2%) in the IR regions, and 48 (16.5%) in the SSC region (Fig.b, c). This distribution pattern indicates that SSRs are enriched in the LSC region, consistent with its higher sequence variability. Analysis of dispersed repeats identified 98 repeats in thechloroplast genome, comprising 12 inverted repeats, 43 palindromic repeats, and 43 forward repeats. No complementary repeats were detected (Supplementary Table S3). The lengths of these repeat sequences ranged from 30 to 27,080 bp, with 48 repeats measuring between 30 and 39 bp, 49 repeats between 40 and 107 bp, and only one repeat measuring 27,080 bp (Fig.).

Analysis of IR/SC expansion and contraction

In this study, we analyzed and compared the boundaries between the inverted repeat (IR) and single-copy (SC) regions in the chloroplast genomes of representative species from nine genera within Phyllanthaceae, and examined variations in gene content and flanking sequences. Although chloroplast genomes across Phyllanthaceae are generally conserved in terms of gene number and sequence organization, significant differences were observed at the IR/SC boundaries among the 11 species examined (Fig.). Analysis of the junction regions revealed considerable structural variation in the chloroplast genomes across the four junction zones (LSC/IRb, IRb/SSC, SSC/IRa, and IRa/LSC), resulting from expansion and contraction of the inverted repeat and single-copy boundaries. In, size of the chloroplast genome of.exhibited high similarity with that of., while structural differences were detected in the LSC and SSC regions of., including partial deletions and gene presence/absence. A notable disparity was also observed in the distance ofto the IRb/LSC boundary. Intergeneric comparisons revealed additional distinctions: for example,is located within the LSC region in, but resides in the IRa region in. These findings collectively indicate the occurrence of IR contraction and expansion in the chloroplast genomes across Phyllanthaceae species.

Chloroplast biogenesis proceeds through three coordinated phases—plastid DNA replication, establishment of the plastid genetic system, and assembly of the photosynthetic apparatus—orchestrated by the nucleus-encoded (NEP) and plastid-encoded (PEP) RNA polymerases. NEP primarily transcribes genes involved in early chloroplast development, whereas PEP, a multi-subunit enzyme encoded by chloroplast rpo-genes, drives the expression of photosynthetic genes and plastid tRNAs. Plastid genes are hierarchically categorized into three classes based on polymerase usage: Class I (PEP-only), Class II (NEP and PEP), and Class III (NEP-only), with PEP (e.g.,,,,) activity being essential for light-responsive gene expression and overall chloroplast integrity. Repetitive sequences and simple sequence repeats (SSRs) are widespread components of plant chloroplast genomes, with variation in SSR abundance and motif types observed both among individual classes and across genera. The distribution, frequency, and genomic positions of these repeats differ among species, rendering them valuable as molecular markers for identifying mutation hotspots and inferring phylogenetic relationships. In this study, we conducted a systematic analysis of codon composition and usage bias in the chloroplast genome of. Our results reveal a pronounced codon usage bias, with a preference for codons ending in A/T. This A/T-rich codon bias is consistent with patterns reported in the chloroplast genomes of numerous other plant species, including,,(Menispermaceae),,, and several members of Phyllanthaceae (,, and,,). In, except for Met and Trp, all amino acids exhibit multiple synonymous codons that typically differ solely in the third position and tend to terminate with A or U. Within these chloroplast genomes, Leu, Arg, and Ser are encoded by six codons, this order of priority aligns consistently with previous findings. Codon usage bias is an important means to effectively analyze the evolutionary characteristics of prokaryotes and eukaryotes, which plays a vital role in molecular biology studies.

Such conservation may reflect the common evolutionary origin of chloroplasts and suggests that codon usage patterns in chloroplast genomes have remained relatively stable across plant lineages. Additionally, most repeated sequences are located within gene regions, underscoring the role of the chloroplast genome in preserving genetic information. Due to their high variability and neutrality, SSRs are widely employed in phylogenetic reconstruction, assessments of genetic diversity, and species identification. Investigating the A/T-biased distribution of chloroplast SSRs within Phyllanthaceae could aid in genus-level classification. The chloroplast genome ofcontains 291 SSRs, predominantly situated in the LSC region, aligning with observations in other Phyllanthaceae species as,, and. Whether these repetitive sequences and SSRs can serve as reliable markers for phylogenetic analyses within Phyllanthaceae warrants further investigation.

Due to the degeneracy of codons, each amino acid corresponds to a minimum of one codon and a maximum of eight codons. There are great differences in the utilization of genome codons in different species and different organisms. This inequality in the use of Synonymous Codon is called RSCU. Codon usage bias (CUB) describes differences in the frequency of multiple synonymous codons that code for the same amino acid. This preference is unique to different species or even different genes within the same species, and is the comprehensive result of natural selection, species mutation and genetic drift during the long-term evolution of genes and species.

Synonymous mutations occur when a base mutation results in an unmodified amino acid; Otherwise, it is a non-synonymous mutation, and non-synonymous mutations are often subject to natural selection. The number of nonsynonymous substitutions per nonsynonymous site (Ka) and the number of synonymous substitutions per synonymous site (Ks) are usually used to represent the effect of selection on a gene. When Ka/Ks is greater than 1, there is a positive selection effect, and when Ka/Ks is less than 1, there is a purifying selection. In the comparison ofwith other species, most of the coding genes (71 out of 76) had Ka/Ks values less than 1, or Ka/Ks values of zero and could not be determined, indicating that they were under purifying selection. These phylogenetic relationships are consistent with those established through classical taxonomic approaches. These results clearly reflect the evolutionary history of Euphorbiaceae and Phyllanthaceae and provide a foundation for further research on the genetic background of Phyllanthaceae, conservation and utilization of germplasm resources, as well as studies on plant phylogenetics and evolution.

Methods

Experimental material

Fresh and young leaves ofandwere collected from Huanghai National Forest Park, Dongtai city, Yancheng city, Jiangsu Province (E120°47′16.47″, N32°51′10.40″) and Gushan, Fuzhou, Fujian Provence (E119°23’14”, N26°3’32”), respectively. The leaves were immediately flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80 ℃. Taxonomic identification was confirmed by Professor Chuanyuan Deng (Fujian Agriculture and Forestry University). Voucher specimens (YB-2024-1−3) are deposited at Yancheng Teachers University, China.

DNA extraction and sequencing

Total genomic DNA was extracted from leaf materials using a Plant Genomic DNA Kit (Tiangen Biotech, China) following the manufacturer’s protocol. DNA concentration and purity were measured using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer. Paired-end (PE) sequencing libraries were constructed following the standard Illumina protocol, including sample quality inspection, fragmentation by ultrasonication, end-repair, adapter ligation, and size selection. Library quality was assessed, and sequencing was performed on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform (Illumina, USA) with 150 bp read length.

Chloroplast genome assembly and annotation analyses

Raw sequencing data were filtered using Fastp v0.20.0() to remove adapters and low-quality reads. The filtered reads were aligned to a custom chloroplast genome reference database using Bowtie2 v2.2.4in very-sensitive-local mode. This reference database was constructed in-house by assembling and annotating chloroplast genomes from key Phyllanthaceae and Euphorbiaceae species (e.g.,,,, etc.) retrieved from NCBI. Reads mapping to the reference were identified as chloroplast DNA.assembly of the chloroplast genome was performed using SPAdes v3.10.1with k-mer values set to 55, 87, and 121, without reliance on a reference genome. The assembled genome was quality-controlled by comparison with the reference sequence(retrieved from NCBI,). Finally, a circular map of the chloroplast genome was generated using OGDRAW().

To ensure accurate annotation of the chloroplast genome, we employed two complementary approaches. First, coding sequences (CDS) were predicted using Prodigal v2.6.3 (), rRNAs were identified with HMMER v3.1b2, and tRNAs were annotated using ARAGORN v1.2.38 (). Second, we performed BLAST v2.6 () comparisons against annotated chloroplast genomes of closely related species obtained from NCBI. The two annotation sets were integrated and manually curated to resolve discrepancies, remove errors, and accurately determine gene boundaries, resulting in a final high-confidence annotation.

Chloroplast simple sequence repeat (cpSSR) and dispersed repeat analysis

Dispersed repeats constitute another category of repetitive sequences distinct from tandem repeats, characterized by their scattered distribution throughout the genome. Dispersed repeats were identified using vmatch v2.3.0 in combination with Perl scripts, with the following parameters: minimum repeat length = 30 bp, Hamming distance = 3. Four types of repeat structures were detected: forward, palindromic, reverse, and complement. We used MISA v1.0 (MIcroSAtellite identification tool,) software to analyze cpSSR. Parameters were set as follows: 1–8 (8 or more repetitions of a single base), 2–5, 3–3, 4 − 3, 5 − 3, 6 − 3.

Boundary analysis

The chloroplast genome has a circular structure with four junction regions between the inverted repeat (IR) and the single-copy regions: LSC/IRb, IRb/SSC, SSC/IRa, and IRa/LSC. During genome evolution, expansion and contraction of the IR boundaries can result in the migration of genes between the IR and single-copy regions. The junction sites were visualized using the CPJSdraw tool () provided by Genepioneer Biotech. Codon usage bias was often assessed using RSCU and effective codon number (ENC) with the CodonW 1.4.2 software. ENC provides an indication of the codon bias strength, with values ranging between 20 and 61, where ENC values ≤ 35 suggest significant codon bias within genes. Conversely, a higher ENC value indicates weaker codon usage bias. The RSCU value was computed to assess the bias in codon usage among genes by comparing the observed frequency of a synonymous codon with its expected frequency. An RSCU value greater than 1 for a specific codon indicates that this codon is being used at a relatively higher frequency.

Nucleotide diversity pi analysis

Pi (nucleic acid diversity) can reveal the variation of nucleic acid sequences of different species, and the regions with high variation can provide potential molecular markers for population genetics. MAFFT software (-auto mode) was used for global comparison of homologous gene sequences of different species. Using dnasp5() calculates the pi value for each gene. Using the Mauve() software default parameters for genome alignment.
