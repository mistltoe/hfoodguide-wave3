Introduction

The WHO has acknowledged the significance of nutrition as a metric in the Millennium Development Goal (MDG) aimed at decreasing maternal and under-5-year death rates, particularly in developing nations. An individual’s well-being and overall health are determined by their nutritional status, which depends on their nutrient intake and utilisation. Optimal nutritional status influences energy levels, immune function, and disease prevention. Unfortunately, there is a worrisome global trend in nutrition, with millions of people suffering from malnutrition, leading to numerous health issues and contributing to the overall burden of diseases. Individuals must be mindful of their nutritional intake and make informed decisions to meet their daily nutrient requirements for optimal health.

Despite India’s immense economic growth over the decade, the nutritional status of its population has not seen the desired transformation. Recent research findings indicate that India, despite making significant progress in reducing stunting growth rates over the past decade, continues to have the highest proportion of undernourished individuals globally. India’s position in the Global Hunger Index (GHI) stood at 103 out of 119 countries in 2018, shedding light on the alarming issue of under nutrition. However, incorporating several varieties of fruits, whole grains, vegetables, and proteins into one’s diet and ensuring the availability of these nutritional sources, even in remote rural areas, can effectively address this issue.

Regarding this matter, emphasising the significance of fruits as essential elements of the human diet for providing essential nutrition is crucial. Fruits have been recognised as an important and nourishing nutrient source vital to promote human well-being. In addition to vitamins and minerals, they also offer carbohydrates, proteins, fibres, organic acids, and bioactive phytochemicals like secondary metabolites. Nutritional research has identified wild edible fruits offer nutritious diet and potential for treating various ailments as a source of bioactive compounds and functional food component. These fruits possess remarkable therapeutic potential due to their bioactive components, such as flavonoids and anthocyanin. Additionally, they are crucial in calories and nutrition as they are available in the wild and are easily accessible even for the local rural communities, which, in several cases, are their primary source of traditional healing.

Wild edible fruits have deep cultural roots in specific regions and are cherished as traditional crops. These fruits play a pivotal role in the livelihood generation of local and rural communities, serving as a critical source of essential macronutrients, vitamins and micronutrients and use in traditional medicine. These fruits are highly valued in the areas where access to commercially available nutrient-rich fruits is limited and often unaffordable. Upon conducting a thorough nutritional analysis of these wild fruits, it becomes evident that their nutritional quality often matches and, in certain instances, surpasses that of domesticated varieties of fruits that require extensive cultivation. They provide affordable yet nutritious options for many individuals living in rural regions. Though they possess significant nutritional benefits, numerous wild edible fruits remain undiscovered or underutilised.

The north-east India is renowned for its exceptional biodiversity, vibrant ethnic diversity, and traditional culture. The indigenous communities in the region rely on forests for sustenance, including food, fibre, and animal feed, as well as for the traditional medicinal resources used in their healthcare practices.

Mizoram, situated in India’s north-east region has the largest indigenous population. The tribal people of the state depend on forests for food, fibre, and fodder, and ethno medicines used in traditional healthcare system. In addition to other edible and non-edible plants in the forests, several wild fruits are also present, which the tribal people have been used since ancient times in their traditional medicinal practices to combat various diseases. Within the rural tribal populations, these wild fruits serve as a valuable means of generating sustainable income and ensuring livelihood security. In Mizoram, in addition to other wild edible fruits, fruits such as,,along with,,andare also available abundantly in natural populations which have long been relied upon for their nutritional and medicinal benefits. Despite their historical usage in traditional medicine, due to lack of insufficient documentation of these species, there may be chance of losing significant information about the ethno medicinal qualities of these fruits. There is need for scientific exploration, documentation, domestication and conservation of these valuable natural resources. It is crucial to study thoroughly and document the nutritional values of these fruits and promote these untamed fruits for food and nutritional security.

In light of the aforementioned details, the present research is aimed to document the nutritional composition, functional food components and bioactive phytochemicals of indigenous wild edible fruits in Mizoram, India.

Materials and methods

Collection of sample

Seven unique wild edible fruits including bignay berries (L. Spreng.), freshwater mangrove ((Lour.) Merr.), amla (Gaertn), poimur tenga ((C.B.Clarke) Mez), lanceleaf marble tree (Roxb), Indian hog plum (L.and chebulic myrobalan (Retz.) were collected during 2021–2022 from the forests of villages viz., north Vanlaiphai, Khawlailung, new Champhai, Tanhril, Melthum, Saikhawthlir, Bualpui village of Serchhip, Champhai, Aizawl, Lawngtlai and Lunglei districts of Mizoram, north-east India (Fig.). As these are not endangered/ threatened species, so no geographical coordinates were taken for the samples. The survey and collection of samples was conducted in the primary and secondary forests as well as home gardens. On the basis of preliminary survey, the above mentioned seven wild edible fruits were collected from these villages. Before collecting the fruits, verbal consent were taken from the forests officials as well as the village head, and orchard owners. Since these fruits are very common in north-east India, and local people know it from plant canopy, architecture of the plant, very peculiar fruit shape. But their formal identification and authentication was done by Prof. Tridip Kumar Hazarika of department of Horticulture, Aromatic and Medicinal Plants, Mizoram University. For all the collected species, herbarium was prepared and the voucher specimens were deposited in the department of Horticulture, Aromatic and Medicinal Plants, Mizoram University having deposition numbers viz., MZU/HAMP/22/AB; MZU/HAMP/22/CB; MZU/HAMP/22/EO; MZU/HAMP/22/ES; MZU/HAMP/22/EL; MZU/HAMP/22/SP and MZU/HAMP/22/TC.

Preparation of the sample

After collection, the fruits were washed with ethanol and subsequently removing the contaminants present on the fruit’s surface with distilled water. The fruits were then dehydrated using absorbent paper. Afterwards, the fruits were partitioned into smaller segments and dehydrated at 50–60˚C. Subsequently, the specimens were pulverised into powder, filtered through a 20 μm screen, and placed in a tightly sealed container. The methanol extracts were acquired via a Buchi R-300 rotary evaporator. Subsequently, the samples were analysed to assess their qualities.

Functional food components analysis

Determination of pigments

To quantify the total carotenoid content, 100 mg of fresh samples were ground in mortar and pestle with 5 ml of 80% acetone, which was then centrifuged at 5000 rpm for 5 min and retrieved the supernatant. The total carotenoid content was determined by measuring the supernatant’s absorbance at wavelengths of 664, 648 and 470 nm using a UV vis spectrophotometer.

The anthocyanin content was estimated using procedure. The fresh fruit sample (1 g) was mixed with 10 ml of ethanolic Hydrochloric acid (HCl) and then moved to a 100 ml volumetric flask to reach the desired volume through dilution. The mixture was chilled at a temperature of 4 °C overnight. Afterwards, a 0.2 ml portion was taken from the mixture and then diluted to a final volume of 10 ml. The optical density (O.D.) was subsequently determined at a wavelength of 535 nm using a UV vis spectrophotometer.

For estimation of total chlorophyll, 1gm fresh sample was ground using 20 ml extractant solvent of 80% acetone after which it was centrifuged at 5000 rpm for 5 min at 4 °C and the supernatant was collected. The same process was repeated till the sample became colourless. Subsequently, it was brought up to the final volume of 100 ml using 80% acetone and analysed using a UV-visible spectrophotometer at 663 and 645 nm wavelengths.

Determination of vitamins

To determine the vitamin E content of the fruits, 10 ml of 0.1 N HSOwas added to grounded 0.5 g fresh fruit sample and left overnight. The extract obtained was then filtered from which 1.5 ml of tissue extract was taken along with the standard solution, and water individually transferring them into three separate test tubes using a pipette. 1.5 ml of ethanol was introduced into the test and blank samples, while 1.5 ml of water was added to the standard sample. After centrifugation, 1.5 ml of xylene was added to each tube which was vortexed and centrifuged. The xylene layer was carefully transferred into a separate tube, where 1 ml of the 2,2’-dipyridyl reagent was added, after which it was analysed at a wavelength of 460 nm compared to a blank solution. A volume of 0.33 ml of ferric chloride was introduced into each test tube designated as the blank after 15 min. Subsequently, the resulting liquid on the top was measured using a UV-visible spectrophotometer at 520 nm, with the blank solution as the reference.

The quantification of ascorbic acid in the fruit sample was determined using a spectrophotometric technique described by. The fresh fruit samples were blended in a water bath with metaphosphoric acid, comprised of a 5% concentration of metaphosphoric acid in a 10% acetic acid solution. After filtration, the resulting mixture was treated with a solution containing 85% HSOand 2,4-dinitrophenyl-hydrazine. Afterwards, the samples were placed in an incubator at 60 °C for 60 min. The measurement was performed using a UV-visible spectrophotometer at a wavelength of 520 nm.

Determination of bioactive-alkaloid and saponin compounds

The approach outlined bywas utilised to estimate the total alkaloid content of the fruits. The dehydrated sample was soaked in 80% ethanol, evaporating at 72 °C to prepare a concentrated solution. Plant extract (1 mg) was dissolved in dimethyl sulphoxide (DMSO), and 1 ml of 2 N HCl was added. Then, the solution was filtered and put into a separate funnel. Subsequently, equal amounts of bromocresol green (5 ml) and phosphate buffer (5 ml) were introduced. Chloroform was introduced and agitated before being placed into a 10 cc flask, and the volume was made up by using chloroform. Multiple reference standard solutions were created, each with varying doses of atropine. The clear liquid was analysed using a UV-visible spectrophotometer at 470 nm, with the reagent blank as the reference.

The total saponin content was determined using the method described bybased on the vanillin-sulphuric acid colorimetric reaction with slight modifications. 50 ml of methanolic plant extract was mixed with 250 ml of distilled water, followed by the addition of vanillin reagent (250 µl), where 800 mg of vanillin was dissolved in 10 ml of 99.5% ethanol. A 2.5 ml aliquot of 72% sulphuric acid was introduced into the mixture and then homogeneously blended. In a water bath, the solution was kept at 60 °C for 10 min and chilled for 10 min in cold water, and the reading was taken at 544 nm. The results were quantified by using diosgenin equivalent (mg DE/g extract) using diosgenin as the standard.

Mineral contents

For estimation of minerals, 1 g of finely ground dried fruit samples were taken into a small beaker. A di-acid combination of HNOand 70% HClOwas added to the solution in a 4:1 ratio (10 ml) which was left undisturbed for 24 h. The extract was then heated using controlled heating with a hot plate until the red NOvapours stopped and became colourless. The final extract was then transferred to a 50 ml beaker and filtered using Whatman No. 1 filter paper and the volume made up was done. The minerals Ca, Co, Na, Fe, Cu, Mg, Mn, K, and Zn were estimated using Atomic Absorption Spectroscopy (AAS) from the extracted sample solution. Automatic Ndigestion, distillation Kjeldahl apparatus was used to estimate the N, and the spectrophotometric vanadium phosphomolybdate technique was employed to assess P.

Nutritional component analysis

The fruits’ moisture, ash, total sugar, crude fibre, reducing sugar, dry matter, non-reducing sugar, fat, hemicellulose, lignin, cellulose, carbohydrate and starch content were measured by using.

To estimate the titratable acidity of the fruits juice, 0.1 N NaOH solution was used for titration. The indicator phenolphthalein identified a pale pink colour, indicating the end point and the acidity was expressed as a percentage in relation to the citric acid concentration. The measurement of TSS content in the fresh fruit was done using a digital refractometer with a range of 0–85 °Brix. A small amount of juice from the fruit was dropped to the prism surface, and the measurement was recorded.

The protein content was evaluated using the method. A total of 0.1 ml and 0.2 ml of dried samples were collected in a test tube. Ensuring that the volume in all test tubes is precisely calibrated to 1 ml. Add 5 ml of reagent C to every tube, including the control tube. Thoroughly mix the components and let the mixture rest for 10 min. Next, add 0.5 ml of Folin reagent to the mixture, ensuring it is mixed well. Then, incubate the solution at room temperature in a light-protected setting for 30 min. The optical absorbance at a wavelength of 660 nm was used to measure the blue hue.

Quantification of anti-nutrients

The approach published bywas used to quantify phytic acid by using the dry samples. The fruits were extracted by using 5% HSOsolution and 1 ml of the supernatant was combined with an equal volume of ferric (III) chloride, which was kept in water bath for 30 min at 100 °C, then kept for 15 minutes in ice-cold water, and then centrifuged at 4000 rpm for 30 min. Then 1 ml of supernatant was carefully placed into a cuvette, followed by the addition of a small quantity of 2,2’bipyridine solution until the appearance of a vivid pink hue. The measurement of absorbance for the reaction mixture was assessed at 519 nm wavelength by using distilled water as reference.

Titrimetric technique ofwas used for estimation of oxalate. A 2 g dry sample was subjected to digestion by using 10 ml of 6 M HCl for one hour and them the mixture was allowed to cool. It is subjected to filtration after being transferred into a volumetric flask (250 ml). In a beaker, 125 ml of filtrate was added 3 to 4 drops of methyl red. NHOH was slowly added to the solution until the colour changes from salmon pink to pale yellow. Afterwards, the solution was measured for the pH level and heated at 90 °C, followed by a cooling process and filtration to eliminate the residue. Once again, the filtered liquid was heated at 90 °C. Subsequently, a 10 ml solution of 5% calcium chloride (CaCl) was added while stirring. The decantation procedure effectively separated the solution, leaving behind a solid substance dissolved in 20% (v/v) HSO(10 ml). The filtrate volume made up to 300 ml, out of which 125 ml of this filtrate was heated near the boiling point. The heated filtrate was titrated against 0.05 M potassium tetraoxomanganate KMNOsolution. A persistent pink colouration for 30 s was observed at end point.

The tannin was evaluated using the Folin-Denis technique developed by. 75 ml of water was added to 0.5 g dried sample in a conical flask, boiled for 30 min, and centrifuged at 2000 rpm for 20 min. The supernatant was carefully poured into a volumetric flask, diluted with distilled water to make a final volume of 100 ml, of which 1 ml was taken in a volumetric flask containing 75 ml of water. Folin-Denis reagent (5 ml) and sodium carbonate (10 ml) were added and then diluted to a final volume of 100 ml. After 30 min using UV-visible spectrophotometer, the absorbance was taken at 700 nm; the blank solution was used for reference.

Data analysis

The data were analysed statistically for ANOVA using SPSS 25.0 (IBM Corp., 2017). The data were represented as the average (mean) and the measure of variability (standard deviation). The Duncan Multiple Range Test (DMRT) was used to differentiate treatment means with a significance threshold of 5% (< 0.05).

Functional food components of seven wild edible fruits

Functional food components are biologically active chemicals in fruits and vegetables that provide benefits beyond essential nutrition. They possess the capacity to provide further health advantages. These compounds, like vitamins, dietary fibres, minerals, probiotics, and phytochemicals, are naturally present in vegetables and fruits which are essential for maintaining optimal health and avoiding illnesses.

Carotenoids are vital for human health because they are advantageous as potent antioxidants, safeguarding cells from harm produced by free radicals. Certain carotenoids, notably β-carotene, serve as essential pro-vitamin A substances that are enzymatically transformed into retinol (vitamin A) in the human body and are crucial for preserving normal vision, boosting immune function, aiding cellular growth and differentiation, and controlling reproductive and developmental activities. Moreover, carotenoids possess strong antioxidant properties that help neutralize reactive oxygen species, which diminishes oxidative stress and aids in the prevention of several chronic and degenerative diseases. Composition of mineral in wild edible fruits

Minerals are crucial constituent of our meals. They serve diverse purposes, supporting our bones, affecting the functioning of muscles and nerves, and maintaining the body’s water equilibrium. Additionally, they serve as constituents of enzymes, hormones, and other physiologically active substances. Certain minerals also play a crucial role in ensuring the immune system functions well. This pertains to both the intrinsic defence system and the adaptive immune response. The availability of minerals may impact both infection susceptibility and chronic illness development. Micronutrient deficiencies pose a substantial public health issue in several developing nations, particularly endangering new borns and pregnant women.

Nitrogen is a crucial constituent of amino acids, which serve as the foundational units of proteins. Proteins are crucial in developing, restoring, and maintaining bodily tissues. Nitrogen is an essential component of nucleotides, constituting DNA and RNA’s building blocks. These molecules are crucial in storing and transmitting genetic information. Conclusion

The current study emphasizes the extraordinary nutritional and phytochemical properties of wild edible fruits from Mizoram, India, highlighting their potential as functional foods with significant health benefits. The studied species showed unique nutritional profiles, highlighting their combined importance in enhancing dietary variety, increasing micronutrient content, and meeting the rising need for natural bioactive substances. Among the fruits assessed,emerged as the most nutritionally exceptional species, showing remarkably high levels essential functional and nutritional components, such as carotenoids, alkaloids, carbohydrates, proteins, energy, potassium, and cobalt, while maintaining lower levels of antinutritional tannins. Similarly,,,,,, andshowcased species-specific enhancement of beneficial phytochemicals.

Together, these results identify wild edible fruits from north-east India as an overlooked source of functional biomolecules with significant potential for use in nutraceutical, pharmaceutical, and functional food sectors. Their consistent inclusion in the diet could play a substantial role in addressing micronutrient deficiencies and lowering the likelihood of chronic degenerative diseases through natural antioxidant and bioactive processes. In addition to their nutritional significance, these species serve as a vital aspect of indigenous food systems and customary ethnobotanical knowledge.

The research emphasizes the need for systematic conservation, scientific evaluation, and sustainable use of these overlooked bioresources. Strategic initiatives focused on community awareness, domestication, value addition, and commercialization could improve rural livelihoods, bolster regional food and nutritional security, and aid in biodiversity conservation within the Indo-Burma biodiversity hotspot. Ultimately, these findings underscore the critical importance of conserving and utilizing local wild flora to combat hidden hunger and support sustainable food security initiatives in the region. Subsequent research concentrating on bioavailability, pharmacological effectiveness, metabolomic analysis, and clinical verification of these bioactive compounds will further reveal the therapeutic and industrial capabilities of these wild edible fruits, facilitating their incorporation into evidence-supported functional nutrition and sustainable health frameworks.
